Lecture 1 — Gravitation & the Keplerian orbit

1 · Kepler's empirical laws

Between 1609 and 1619, working from Tycho Brahe's naked-eye observations (accurate to about one arc-minute, the best of the pre-telescopic era), Johannes Kepler distilled three descriptive laws of planetary motion — especially from the stubborn orbit of Mars, whose eccentricity finally forced him to abandon circles.

LawStatementModern form
I — EllipsesEach planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.$r=\dfrac{p}{1+e\cos\nu}$
II — Equal areasThe Sun–planet line sweeps equal areas in equal times.$\dfrac{dA}{dt}=\dfrac{h}{2}=\text{const}$
III — Harmonic lawThe square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis.$T^{2}=\dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\mu}a^{3}$
What they are — and aren't. Kepler's laws are purely kinematic: a compact description of what the planets do. They contain no force and no mass. Supplying the why — and thereby deriving all three from a single force law — was Newton's achievement, and it is the arc of this week. We meet them here as facts; by §10 we prove them.

2 · Newton's laws of motion

Three axioms underpin the entire dynamical treatment:

  • First (inertia). A body maintains constant velocity unless a net force acts. This defines the class of inertial frames in which our equations are valid — for Earth-orbiting satellites, the Earth-Centred Inertial (ECI) frame.
  • Second. $\mathbf F=\dfrac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=m\mathbf a$ for constant mass. Applied to gravity, this is the equation of motion.
  • Third (action–reaction). The two bodies exert equal and opposite forces. This symmetry produces the two-body reduction and the uniform motion of the centre of mass.
Where each enters. The first law fixes the reference frame; the second builds the differential equation $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\mu\mathbf r/r^3$; the third delivers momentum conservation and lets us collapse two equations into one (§5).

3 · Universal gravitation & the Moon test

$$\mathbf F=-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^{2}}\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad G=6.674\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}.$$

Newton's radical claim was universality: the same inverse-square force that makes an apple fall holds the Moon in its orbit. The apple and the Moon are both "falling"; they differ only in distance. He checked this quantitatively — the celebrated Moon test.

— comparing the Moon's acceleration to surface gravity

If gravity falls off as $1/r^2$, the Moon's acceleration should be surface gravity $g$ scaled by $(R_\oplus/r_{\rm M})^2$:

$$a_{\rm pred}=g\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{r_{\rm M}}\right)^{2}=9.81\left(\frac{6371}{384\,400}\right)^{2}=9.81\,(2.75\times10^{-4})=2.70\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Independently, the Moon's centripetal acceleration on its (nearly circular) orbit of period $T=27.32$ d is

$$a_{\rm obs}=\omega^{2}r_{\rm M}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^{2}r_{\rm M}=(2.66\times10^{-6})^{2}(3.844\times10^{8})=2.72\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

The two agree to about 1% — a striking confirmation that the same law governs terrestrial and celestial motion. (The small residual is largely the Earth–Moon barycentre offset.)

The unification. One force law, tested by one comparison, merged terrestrial and celestial mechanics into a single predictive science. Everything downstream — orbits, perturbations, transfers, interplanetary trajectories — is a consequence.

4 · Gravitational potential & energy

$$U(r)=-\frac{\mu}{r},\qquad \mathbf g=-\nabla U=-\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}\hat{\mathbf r}.$$

The specific potential energy (per unit mass) is $-\mu/r$, taken zero at infinity and negative everywhere finite — a potential well. Because the field is the gradient of a scalar, gravity is conservative: the work it does is path-independent, so mechanical energy is conserved (proved in §6). Climbing the well to $U=0$ is precisely escape.

— escape velocity from the energy balance

Escape means reaching $r\to\infty$ with just-vanishing speed, i.e. total specific energy $\varepsilon=0$:

$$\tfrac12 v_{\rm esc}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_{\rm circ}.$$

Escape speed is always $\sqrt2$ times the local circular speed — a 41% increase, independent of radius.

Why we carry $\mu$, not $G$ and $M$. $G$ is among the least precisely measured constants in physics ($\sim10^{-4}$ relative uncertainty), but the product $\mu=GM$ is obtained directly from tracking to nine significant figures. For Earth, $\mu_\oplus=398\,600.4418\ \mathrm{km^3/s^2}$.

5 · The two-body reduction (§3.2.1)

Two point masses interact only through gravity. We collapse the two equations of motion into a single equation for the relative position $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1$.

$$\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}\,\mathbf r=\mathbf 0,\qquad \mu\equiv G(m_1+m_2)\ \xrightarrow{\,m_1\gg m_2\,}\ GM.$$
— from two bodies to one relative equation

The force on each body lies along $\mathbf r$:

$$m_1\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=+\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^{3}}\mathbf r,\qquad m_2\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^{3}}\mathbf r.$$

Divide each by its mass and subtract:

$$\ddot{\mathbf r}=\ddot{\mathbf r}_2-\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=-\frac{G(m_1+m_2)}{r^{3}}\mathbf r,$$

which is the boxed equation with $\mu=G(m_1+m_2)$. When $m_1\gg m_2$, $\mu\to GM_1$.

Centre of mass. Adding the mass-weighted equations gives $m_1\ddot{\mathbf r}_1+m_2\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=\mathbf 0$, so $\ddot{\mathbf R}_{\rm cm}=\mathbf 0$: the barycentre moves uniformly and we may work in its inertial frame.

Reduced mass. The relative motion equals that of one particle of mass $\mu_r=\dfrac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}$ in the fixed potential $-Gm_1m_2/r$. Dividing its energy and momentum by $\mu_r$ yields the specific quantities $\varepsilon,\mathbf h$ — hence $\mu=G(m_1+m_2)$.

6 · Conserved quantities & the effective potential (§3.2.2)

6.1 Specific angular momentum

$$\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}=\text{const}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{planar motion},\quad \frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{h}{2}.$$
— central force means no torque
$$\dot{\mathbf h}=\dot{\mathbf r}\times\dot{\mathbf r}+\mathbf r\times\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf 0+\mathbf r\times\!\Big(-\tfrac{\mu}{r^{3}}\mathbf r\Big)=\mathbf 0.$$

So $\mathbf h$ is constant. Since $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf h=0$ at all times, $\mathbf r$ stays in the plane perpendicular to the fixed $\mathbf h$. The swept area is $dA=\tfrac12|\mathbf r\times d\mathbf r|=\tfrac12 h\,dt$, giving Kepler's second law.

6.2 Specific mechanical energy

$$\varepsilon=\tfrac12 v^{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=\text{const}.$$
— the energy integral

Dot the equation of motion with $\dot{\mathbf r}$ and use $\mathbf r\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}=r\dot r$:

$$\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}(r\dot r)=\frac{d}{dt}\!\Big(\tfrac12 v^{2}\Big)-\frac{d}{dt}\!\Big(\frac{\mu}{r}\Big)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \varepsilon=\text{const}.$$

6.3 The effective potential

Writing $v^{2}=\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\nu^{2}$ and using $h=r^{2}\dot\nu$, the transverse motion becomes an $h^{2}/r^{2}$ term and the radial motion is one-dimensional:

$$\varepsilon=\tfrac12\dot r^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{h^{2}}{2r^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{r}}_{U_{\text{eff}}(r)}.$$

The $h^{2}/2r^{2}$ centrifugal barrier repels; $-\mu/r$ attracts. Turning points ($\dot r=0$) are where the energy line $\varepsilon$ meets $U_{\text{eff}}$: two crossings → ellipse, tangency at the minimum → circle, one crossing → hyperbola, $\varepsilon=0$ → parabola.

7 · The orbit equation (§3.2.3)

$$r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\nu},\qquad p=\frac{h^{2}}{\mu}=a(1-e^{2}).$$

7.1 Route A — the Binet substitution

— change variable to u = 1/r

With $u=1/r$ and $h=r^{2}\dot\nu$, one finds $\dot r=-h\,du/d\nu$ and $\ddot r=-h^{2}u^{2}\,d^{2}u/d\nu^{2}$. Substituting into the radial equation $\ddot r-r\dot\nu^{2}=-\mu/r^{2}$:

$$-h^{2}u^{2}u''-h^{2}u^{3}=-\mu u^{2}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{u''+u=\frac{\mu}{h^{2}}}.$$

The solution $u=\dfrac{\mu}{h^{2}}(1+e\cos\nu)$ (perigee at $\nu=0$) inverts to $r=\dfrac{p}{1+e\cos\nu}$ with $p=h^{2}/\mu$.

7.2 Route B — the eccentricity (Laplace–Runge–Lenz) vector

— a constant vector pointing to perigee

Define $\mathbf e=\dfrac{\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf h}{\mu}-\hat{\mathbf r}$. One shows $\dot{\mathbf e}=\mathbf 0$. Dotting with $\mathbf r$ and using $\mathbf r\cdot(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf h)=\mathbf h\cdot(\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r})=h^{2}$:

$$\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf e=\frac{h^{2}}{\mu}-r=p-r.$$

With $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf e=re\cos\nu$ this gives $r=\dfrac{p}{1+e\cos\nu}$; $|\mathbf e|=e$ and $\mathbf e$ points toward perigee.

Energy ↔ shape. Combining $p=a(1-e^{2})$ with $\varepsilon=-\mu/2a$ (§9) gives $e^{2}=1+\dfrac{2\varepsilon h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}$ — so the sign of the energy fixes the conic type.

8.2 Hyperbolic orbits — the interplanetary workhorse

For $e>1$ the orbit is open: the body arrives from and departs to infinity along two asymptotes, retaining a residual speed $v_\infty$. Here $a<0$, and the specific energy is positive.

$$\varepsilon=-\frac{\mu}{2a}>0,\qquad v_\infty^{2}=2\varepsilon=-\frac{\mu}{a}=\frac{\mu}{|a|}\equiv C_3.$$
— excess speed, eccentricity, and turn angle

Excess speed. As $r\to\infty$, vis-viva ($v^2=\mu(2/r-1/a)$) gives $v_\infty^{2}=-\mu/a=\mu/|a|$. The characteristic energy $C_3\equiv v_\infty^{2}=2\varepsilon$ is the quantity launch vehicles are rated by.

Eccentricity from the flyby. At perigee, $r_p=|a|(e-1)$ and $|a|=\mu/v_\infty^{2}$, so

$$r_p=\frac{\mu}{v_\infty^{2}}(e-1)\ \Longrightarrow\ e=1+\frac{r_p\,v_\infty^{2}}{\mu}.$$

Turn (deflection) angle. The asymptote lies at the true anomaly $\nu_\infty$ where $r\to\infty$, i.e. $1+e\cos\nu_\infty=0$, so $\cos\nu_\infty=-1/e$. The velocity direction rotates by $\delta$ with $\delta/2=\nu_\infty-90^\circ$, hence

$$\sin\frac{\delta}{2}=-\cos\nu_\infty=\frac{1}{e}.$$

A close, slow pass (small $r_p$, small $v_\infty$) gives $e\to1$ and a large deflection — the essence of a gravity assist (Week 5).

The four conics by energy. circle/ellipse: $a>0,\ \varepsilon<0,\ C_3<0$ (bound); parabola: $a\to\infty,\ \varepsilon=0,\ C_3=0$ (escape with $v\to0$); hyperbola: $a<0,\ \varepsilon>0,\ C_3>0$ (escape with $v_\infty$). One equation, one energy scale.

Lecture 2 — Energy, geometry & time

9 · Vis-viva, energy & the flight-path angle (§3.2.4)

$$v^{2}=\mu\!\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right),\qquad \varepsilon=-\frac{\mu}{2a}.$$
— energy depends only on a

Evaluate the energy at perigee ($\mathbf v\perp\mathbf r$, $v_p=h/r_p$, $r_p=a(1-e)$, $h^2=\mu a(1-e^2)$):

$$\varepsilon=\tfrac12\frac{h^{2}}{r_p^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{r_p}=\frac{\mu}{a(1-e)}\!\left[\frac{1+e}{2}-1\right]=-\frac{\mu}{2a}.$$

Equating to $\tfrac12 v^2-\mu/r$ at any point gives vis-viva. Circular ($a=r$): $v=\sqrt{\mu/r}$; parabolic ($a\to\infty$): $v=\sqrt{2\mu/r}$.

9.1 Flight-path angle

$$\tan\gamma=\frac{v_r}{v_\perp}=\frac{e\sin\nu}{1+e\cos\nu}.$$
— from differentiating the orbit equation

Differentiating $r=p/(1+e\cos\nu)$ gives $\dot r=\dfrac{re\sin\nu}{1+e\cos\nu}\dot\nu$. With $v_r=\dot r$ and $v_\perp=r\dot\nu$, the ratio is the boxed result. $\gamma=0$ at both apsides (velocity purely horizontal), largest near $\nu=90^\circ$.

The workhorse. Vis-viva gives speed from just $r$ and $a$ — eccentricity never enters the magnitude. Every Δv in Week 5 is "vis-viva before, vis-viva after, take the difference." Because $\varepsilon=-\mu/2a$, a higher orbit is more energetic yet slower.

10 · Kepler's laws derived · the harmonic law (§3.2.5)

We now close the loop begun in §1: all three empirical laws follow from $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\mu\mathbf r/r^{3}$.

  • First law. The orbit equation $r=p/(1+e\cos\nu)$ with $0\le e<1$ is an ellipse with the primary at a focus (§7).
  • Second law. $dA/dt=h/2=\text{const}$ (§6.1) — equal areas in equal times.
  • Third law. The period depends only on $a$:
$$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{\mu}},\qquad n=\frac{2\pi}{T}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a^{3}}}.$$
— integrating the areal rate over one period

Over one period the radius sweeps the whole ellipse, area $A=\pi ab$. Since $dA/dt=h/2$ is constant, $A=\tfrac{h}{2}T$, so $T=2\pi ab/h$. Insert $b=a\sqrt{1-e^{2}}$ and $h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^{2})}$:

$$T=\frac{2\pi\,a^{2}\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^{2})}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{\mu}}.$$

The eccentricity cancels: two orbits with equal $a$ share a period regardless of shape — the basis of formation flying.

10.1 The harmonic law — weighing worlds

$$T^{2}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\mu}a^{3}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu=\frac{4\pi^{2}a^{3}}{T^{2}},\quad M=\frac{\mu}{G}.$$

About a given body the ratio $a^{3}/T^{2}$ is fixed, so a single orbit calibrates all others (on a log–log plot, $\log T$ versus $\log a$ is a straight line of slope $3/2$). Turned around, measuring any satellite's $a$ and $T$ yields the central body's $\mu$ — and hence its mass. This is how we weigh the Earth, the Sun, the planets, and stars with planets.

Check with the Moon. $a=384\,400$ km, $T=27.32$ d $=2.361\times10^{6}$ s: $\ \mu=4\pi^{2}(3.844\times10^{5})^{3}/(2.361\times10^{6})^{2}\approx 4.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{km^3/s^2}$ — Earth's $\mu$, recovered from the Moon alone.

10.2 Kepler ⟷ Newton: a two-way equivalence

Newton's achievement was to show the empirical laws and the force law are the same statement. In one direction: equal areas (Law II) implies the force is central; an ellipse with the force at a focus implies it varies as $1/r^{2}$; and the harmonic law implies the same constant $\mu$ for every orbit. In the other direction, we have just derived all three laws from $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\mu\mathbf r/r^{3}$. Prompted by Halley's question — "what path does an inverse-square force produce?" — this equivalence launched the Principia and predictive celestial mechanics.

11 · Coordinate frames & the perifocal state

Elements and state vectors live in an inertial frame. Two frames matter now:

  • ECI $(\hat{\mathbf I},\hat{\mathbf J},\hat{\mathbf K})$ — $\hat{\mathbf I}$ toward the First Point of Aries, $\hat{\mathbf K}$ along Earth's spin axis. Elements are quoted here so they do not rotate with the Earth.
  • Perifocal (PQW) $(\hat{\mathbf P},\hat{\mathbf Q},\hat{\mathbf W})$ — in the orbit plane, $\hat{\mathbf P}$ toward perigee, $\hat{\mathbf W}$ along $\mathbf h$. The natural frame for writing $\mathbf r,\mathbf v$.
$$\mathbf r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\nu}\big(\cos\nu\,\hat{\mathbf P}+\sin\nu\,\hat{\mathbf Q}\big),\quad \mathbf v=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{p}}\big[-\sin\nu\,\hat{\mathbf P}+(e+\cos\nu)\,\hat{\mathbf Q}\big].$$
— the perifocal velocity

Differentiate $\mathbf r=r(\cos\nu\,\hat{\mathbf P}+\sin\nu\,\hat{\mathbf Q})$ and use $\dot\nu=h/r^{2}$, $h=\sqrt{\mu p}$, and $\dot r=\dfrac{re\sin\nu}{1+e\cos\nu}\dot\nu$. After collecting terms the radial parts combine to give

$$\mathbf v=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{p}}\big[-\sin\nu\,\hat{\mathbf P}+(e+\cos\nu)\,\hat{\mathbf Q}\big].$$

The transformation to ECI is the 3-1-3 rotation by the orientation angles:

$$\mathbf r_{\rm ECI}=R_3(-\Omega)\,R_1(-i)\,R_3(-\omega)\,\mathbf r_{\rm PQW},$$

assembled explicitly in Week 4.

12 · The problem of time — a bridge to Week 4

The orbit equation gives the shape $r(\nu)$ and the constants fix the size, but not when the body is at a given $\nu$. Because $\dot\nu=h/r^{2}$ varies around the orbit, $\nu(t)$ is nonlinear and has no elementary inverse.

$$M=n(t-t_p),\qquad M=E-e\sin E,\qquad \tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}.$$

The device (Week 4) is to introduce the eccentric anomaly $E$, integrate the area to obtain the mean anomaly $M$ advancing uniformly in time at rate $n=\sqrt{\mu/a^{3}}$ (from the third law), and solve the transcendental Kepler equation $M=E-e\sin E$ numerically. That machinery — the anomalies, Newton's-method solution, and the full time-of-flight — opens Week 4.

P3 · Escape and the √2 rule

Show $v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt2\,v_{\rm circ}$ and evaluate at $r=7000$ km.

P4 · a, h, e from a state vector

$r=8000$ km, $v=8.5$ km/s, $\gamma=20^\circ$. Find $a,h,e$.

$\varepsilon=\tfrac12 v^{2}-\mu/r=-13.70\Rightarrow a=-\mu/2\varepsilon=\mathbf{14\,547}$ km. $h=rv\cos\gamma=63\,889\Rightarrow p=h^2/\mu=10\,240$ km. $e=\sqrt{1-p/a}=\mathbf{0.544}$.

P5 · Weigh the Earth from the Moon

Using $a=384\,400$ km and $T=27.32$ d, estimate $\mu_\oplus$ and $M_\oplus$.

$\mu=4\pi^{2}a^{3}/T^{2}=4\pi^{2}(3.844\times10^{5})^{3}/(2.361\times10^{6})^{2}\approx\mathbf{4.0\times10^{5}\ km^3/s^2}$. Then $M=\mu/G=(4.0\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2})/(6.674\times10^{-11})\approx\mathbf{6.0\times10^{24}\ kg}$.

P6 · Hyperbolic flyby of Jupiter

$\mu_J=1.267\times10^{8}$, perigee $r_p=142\,984$ km, $v_\infty=6$ km/s. Find $e$, turn angle $\delta$, and $C_3$.

$e=1+r_p v_\infty^{2}/\mu_J=1+142984(36)/1.267\times10^{8}=\mathbf{1.041}$. $\delta=2\sin^{-1}(1/e)=\mathbf{147^\circ}$. $C_3=v_\infty^{2}=\mathbf{36\ km^2/s^2}$; $a=-\mu_J/C_3=-3.52\times10^{6}$ km.

P7 · Derive it yourself

From $\varepsilon=\tfrac12 v^2-\mu/r$ and $\varepsilon=-\mu/2a$, derive vis-viva; then show $e^{2}=1+2\varepsilon h^{2}/\mu^{2}$.

Equate the two ε expressions → $v^2=\mu(2/r-1/a)$. Then $1-e^2=p/a=h^2/(\mu a)$ and $a=-\mu/2\varepsilon$ give $1-e^2=-2\varepsilon h^2/\mu^2$, i.e. $e^2=1+2\varepsilon h^2/\mu^2$.
