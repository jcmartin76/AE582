Bridge from Week 3
The perfect orbit was only the starting point
$$\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}\,\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad a,e,i,\Omega,\omega\ \text{all constant}$$

What Week 3 delivered

A closed conic that never changes shape or orientation. Five elements are frozen; only the position along the orbit ($\nu$) evolves through Kepler's equation.

What this week adds

Earth is neither a point nor alone. Its bulge, the atmosphere, the Sun and Moon all push on the satellite (L1). Then we ask which orbit a mission actually needs, and the ΔV to reach it (L2).
Two viewpoints recur: a perturbation is a nuisance to be modelled — and, for J₂ precession, a resource to be designed around (Sun‑synchronous orbits, TRACERS).

Week 4 · Lecture 1
The perturbed equation of motion
$$\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}\,\mathbf r=\mathbf b,\qquad \mathbf b=\mathbf a_{J_2}+\mathbf a_{\text{drag}}+\mathbf a_{\text{Sun}}+\mathbf a_{\text{Moon}}+\mathbf a_{\text{SRP}}+\dots$$

Osculating elements

Away from Earth the non‑uniform mass distribution makes the point‑mass force law invalid, and at low altitude the atmosphere adds drag. We keep the Keplerian description but let the "constants" $a,e,i,\Omega,\omega$ change slowly — the osculating elements that instantaneously match the true path.

Lagrange's planetary equations

The standard way to include perturbations is the method of variation of parameters, giving Lagrange's planetary equations for $\dot a,\dot e,\dot i,\dot\Omega,\dot\omega$. Note the caveat: these forms break down for $e=0$ or $i=0$ and need reformulation (equinoctial elements).
We can't solve this in closed form, so we ask how each perturbation moves the elements on average per orbit. The secular (non‑repeating) parts dominate mission design.

Gravitational potential of the Earth
$$U(r,\phi,\lambda)=\frac{\mu}{r}\!\left[1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{r}\Big)^{\!n}\!J_nP_{n0}(\cos\phi)+\sum_{n,m}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{r}\Big)^{\!n}\!(C_{nm}\cos m\lambda+S_{nm}\sin m\lambda)P_{nm}\right]$$

  • Zonal terms $J_n$ reflect mass distribution independent of longitude; tesseral terms $C_{nm},S_{nm}$ capture longitude variation.
  • $J_2$ is three orders of magnitude larger than every other term — it dominates the gravitational perturbations.

The two secular rates from J₂
1
$U_{J_2}=\dfrac{\mu J_2 R_E^2}{2r^3}\big(3\sin^2\phi-1\big)$
Keep only the $n{=}2$ zonal term of the geopotential — the dominant disturbing potential.
2
$\sin\phi=\sin i\,\sin(\omega+\nu)$
Write latitude in orbital elements, then average over one revolution to isolate the secular part.
3
$\boxed{\ \dot\Omega=-\dfrac{3}{2}\,nJ_2\!\left(\dfrac{R_E}{p}\right)^{\!2}\cos i\ }$

4

Week 4 · Lecture 1
Regression of the line of nodes

Why the plane rotates

The equatorial bulge exerts a torque on the orbit's angular‑momentum vector. As the satellite crosses toward the equator it feels an extra pull; averaged over an orbit this makes the whole orbit plane rotate about Earth's spin axis — the ascending node slides along the equator.

Direction & size

For a prograde orbit ($i<90°$) the node moves west (regression); retrograde orbits advance east. The rate grows for low, low‑inclination orbits and is largest near the equator, zero at $i=90°$. Typical LEO: a few degrees per day.
$$\overline{\Omega}=\Omega_0-\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{J_2R_E^2}{p^2}\,\bar n\,t\,\cos i$$
This looks like a pure cost — but choose $i$ so the node keeps pace with Earth's motion around the Sun and it becomes the most useful orbit in remote sensing.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Turning drift into design — Sun‑synchronous orbits
$$\dot\Omega=+0.9856^{\circ}/\text{day}=\frac{360^{\circ}}{365.25\ \text{day}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos i=-\frac{\dot\Omega\,p^2}{1.5\,nJ_2R_E^2}$$

Lock the plane to the Sun

Choose $i$ so the node drifts exactly one turn per year, matching Earth's motion around the Sun. The orbit plane then keeps a fixed angle to the sunline — every pass over a given latitude happens at the same local solar time.

The workhorse orbit

Constant lighting is priceless for imaging and climate sensors (Landsat, Sentinel, most weather sats). It needs a slightly retrograde inclination, typically $i\approx96°\text{–}99°$ in LEO — the minus sign forces $i>90°$.
The next slide makes "locked" concrete: watch the local time at the node hold constant for a Sun‑sync orbit and drift for a fixed one.

Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Precession of the line of apsides & Molniya orbits

The apsides rotate within the plane

As the spacecraft crosses the equator it "sees" the extra pull of the bulge, which rotates the line of apsides (the perigee–apogee line) inside the orbit plane. Unlike a plane change this is an in‑plane rotation: perigee slowly marches around the orbit at the rate $\dot\omega$ from the J₂ derivation.

Freeze it at the critical inclination

Set $5\cos^2 i-1=0\Rightarrow i=63.4°$ (or $116.6°$). Then $\dot\omega=0$ and the apogee stops drifting. Choose any other inclination and the apogee slowly migrates between hemispheres — over a year it would spend part of its time over the wrong one, ruining a fixed‑apogee mission.
$$i_{\text{crit}}=\arccos\!\sqrt{\tfrac15}=63.435^{\circ}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \dot\omega=0$$

Molniya & Tundra

A highly eccentric $i=63.4°$ orbit parks apogee over the far north for hours per pass. This is how the USSR gave high‑latitude regions the coverage a geostationary belt over the equator physically cannot reach.

Who else uses it

Tundra orbits (24 h period, same inclination) serve regional broadcast; GPS sits in a nearby inclination band. The critical inclination is a genuine design constant, not a curiosity.
Nodal regression rotates the plane (out of plane); apsidal precession rotates the ellipse (in plane). J₂ drives both — and both can be turned from nuisance into design tool.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Molniya orbits — engineering the apogee dwell

The design recipe

  • Inclination $i=63.4°$ → $\dot\omega=0$: the apogee is frozen (L1).
  • Argument of perigee $\omega=270°$ → apogee sits at the orbit's northernmost point.
  • High eccentricity $e\approx0.74$ (600 km perigee, ~39 750 km apogee).
  • Period ~12 h → the ground track repeats, with two apogee loops per day.

Why the U.S.S.R. built them

A geostationary belt sits over the equator and barely clears the horizon from high latitudes, so it cannot serve the far north. By Kepler's 2nd law a Molniya satellite crawls near apogee, spending ~8 of its ~12 h hanging over northern latitudes. Three satellites phased 8 h apart give continuous high‑latitude coverage — communications, and later early‑warning.
"Molniya" means lightning. Tundra orbits use the same $63.4°$ inclination at a 24‑h period. See the ground track dwell over the U.S.S.R. on the next slide.

Week 4 · Lecture 1 · non‑conservative
Atmospheric drag
$$\mathbf F_D=\tfrac12\,\rho\,S C_D\,V_r^{2}\left(\frac{-\mathbf V_r}{|V_r|}\right),\qquad \frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau}\approx-3\pi\rho r\,(SC_D/M)$$

Strongest at perigee

For LEO spacecraft drag cannot be ignored. From the drag equation it is largest at perigee, where both density and speed peak. To first order that acts like an impulsive retro‑burn at perigee, lowering the semi‑major axis; in a circular orbit drag acts continuously and the change of period over one revolution is the formula above.

Contraction, then re‑entry

Drag removes energy ($\varepsilon=-\mu/2a$), so $a$ and $e$ shrink — the orbit contracts and circularises, then decays. Because Earth's atmosphere co‑rotates, the drag force also has an out‑of‑plane component, so it slowly changes inclination too. King‑Hele gives the radial/transverse/normal ($S,T,W$) components for a rotating atmosphere.
$V_r$ is velocity relative to the (rotating) atmosphere, $SC_D/M$ the ballistic coefficient, $\rho$ the local density. Solar activity swells $\rho$ and can shorten lifetimes dramatically.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · non‑conservative
Orbital lifetime
$$\tau\sim\frac{e_0^{2}}{2B}\left(1-\frac{11}{6}e_0+\frac{29}{16}e_0^{2}+\frac{7}{8}\frac{H}{a_0}\right),\qquad B\sim\sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{\mu}{a_0^3}\Big)}\,\tfrac{SC_D}{M}\rho_{p0}a_0e_0\,I_1\!\Big(\tfrac{a_0e_0}{H}\Big)e^{-e_0(1+a_0/H)}$$

From injection to re‑entry

Lifetime is the time from orbit injection until aerodynamic drag brings the vehicle back in. Here $e_0,a_0$ are the values when control ceases, $H$ the scale height near perigee, $\rho_{p0}$ the density there, and $I_1$ a modified Bessel function. The expression neglects atmospheric rotation.

Why it matters

Decay is slow high up (years at 600 km) but runs away low down (days near 300 km) because density rises exponentially. Uncontrolled re‑entries — Skylab over Australia, Cosmos 954B over Canada — show the hazard, and modern LEO design must plan a controlled de‑orbit or a <25‑year decay.
We modelled decay interactively in the Week 3 lifetime demo; here it is one perturbation among several, and the input to end‑of‑life planning we return to in Lecture 2.
Week 4 · Lecture 1
Third‑body & solar radiation pressure

Sun & Moon (third body)

Negligible in LEO but dominant at GEO, where they tilt the orbit plane ~0.85°/yr. This is why geostationary satellites spend most of their station‑keeping fuel on north–south (inclination) control — a theme we return to in Week 5.

Solar radiation pressure

Photons carry momentum ($\approx4.6\ \mu$Pa at 1 AU). For high area‑to‑mass craft it grows eccentricity and must be modelled — or harnessed, as in solar sails. Below ~0.005 m²/kg it is minor; above ~0.01 m²/kg it is significant.
Both are mostly periodic near Earth, but their long‑period terms matter for precise, long‑lived missions. For most LEO work: model J₂ + drag, treat these as corrections.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · case study
Precession as a free maneuver — TRACERS
1
The science fixes a Sun‑synchronous plane at a specific local time
TRACERS samples the northern magnetic cusp, which is best crossed from a Sun‑synchronous orbit at a 10:30 (or 02:30) local time of descending node (LTDN) — the plane's orientation to the Sun is a hard requirement.
2
Flying rideshare delivers the wrong local time — 06:00 LTDN
As a secondary payload (launched with PUNCH), TRACERS is dropped into whatever Sun‑sync plane the primary needs: a 06:00 LTDN orbit, several hours of local time away from the science orbit.
3
Rotating the plane directly is impossible
A 4.5‑hour LTDN shift is a ~67° rotation of the orbit plane (a RAAN change). At LEO speed, $\Delta V=2v\sin(\Delta\Omega/2)$ would need several km/s — far more propellant than a SMEX‑class spacecraft carries.
4
Instead: raise the altitude a little, keeping the inclination
A small in‑plane burn lifts the orbit (to ~610 km) while holding the ~590 km inclination. At the higher altitude the orbit is no longer perfectly Sun‑synchronous, so $\dot\Omega$ no longer matches the Sun and the plane drifts in local time.
5
Let it drift ~1 year, then drop back down
After about a year the LTDN has walked to the science value; lowering the altitude re‑establishes Sun‑synchronism at the new local time. Time — not propellant — bought the plane change.
This is the hinge of the week: the same $\dot\Omega$ we derived as a nuisance in L1 becomes the maneuver. The next slide puts numbers to it.

TRACERS — the mission & the numbers

The drift logic

$$\dot\Omega=-\tfrac{3}{2}nJ_2\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{p}\Big)^{2}\cos i$$
Raising $a$ lowers $n$ and raises $p$, so $|\dot\Omega|$ drops below the $0.9856°/$day the Sun needs. The plane slips in local time at the difference rate; integrate it over ~1 year to cover the LTDN gap, then restore $a$ to re‑lock. A small in‑plane $\Delta V$ replaces a multi‑km/s plane change.

Mission analysis — orbit categories
  • Mission design happens in the feasibility study and strongly shapes the spacecraft.
  • It is an iterative process: spacecraft constraints and mission goals trade against each other.
  • After pre‑launch and launch, orbit transfer is the next key event — moving the vehicle from its injection orbit to the mission orbit.

Missions sort by orbit

  1. Low Earth orbit (LEO)
  2. Medium Earth orbit (MEO)
  3. Geostationary (GEO)
  4. Highly elliptical (HEO)
  5. Non‑geocentric (Lagrange, interplanetary)
Transfer carries an a‑priori uncertainty (the launch vehicle's performance envelope) and an a‑posteriori one (trajectory measurement errors) — both must be budgeted.
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · foundations
Altitude sets speed and period
$$v=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{R_E+z}},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\mu}}\,(R_E+z)^{3/2}$$
  • Higher orbits are slower but take longer — the two curves move in opposite directions.
  • Across LEO (0–2000 km): speed falls ~7.9 → 6.9 km/s while period grows ~85 → 127 min.
  • This trade underlies every mission‑orbit choice: coverage, revisit time, launch cost.
Setting $e=0$ in the orbit equation gives $r=h^2/\mu$ and these two relations directly (course, Fig. 2.14). They are the vis‑viva result of Week 3, restated as the mission‑analysis "menu" of circular orbits.
Week 4 · Lecture 2
Mission analysis — propellant drives everything

Transfers and pointing cost fuel

Transferring between orbits needs propellant, and planners size it from the initial and target orbits. Observatories that must re‑point continuously (sky surveys) also spend propellant: reaction wheels slew the spacecraft, and thrusters periodically dump the wheels' angular momentum.

Fuel is the mission clock

A contingency plan covers unscheduled pointing, but careful budgeting is critical — many missions end when fuel runs out, not when hardware fails.
Other observatory constraints when deriving a mission profile: minimum Sun/telescope angle, Earth‑limb/telescope angle, passage through the South Atlantic Anomaly, and the thermal balance of the spacecraft and its sensors.
Week 4 · Lecture 2
Mission analysis — end of life & design drivers

Disposal is part of the design

End of life is a real mission phase. For GEO, longitude slots are scarce, so residual propellant boosts the satellite to a higher graveyard orbit. For LEO, the plan is a controlled re‑entry or a drop to a short‑lifetime disposal orbit (recall the lifetime formula from L1).

Objectives → design drivers

Analysing the mission objectives produces the design drivers — the handful of requirements (orbit, coverage, power, pointing, lifetime) that dictate the overall vehicle configuration. Everything downstream serves them.
The rest of this lecture works the three questions mission analysis must answer: how to reach the orbit (transfers), how much of the Earth it sees (coverage), and when we can launch (windows).
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · orbit transfer
The Keplerian orbit‑transfer problem

One maneuver, or two?

To move between two orbits with a single burn, the initial and final orbits must intersect at the burn point. If they don't — e.g. two non‑touching circular orbits — you need at least two maneuvers.

The Hohmann transfer

The Hohmann transfer uses two burns to move between coplanar circular orbits via an ellipse tangent to both. It is the minimum‑energy two‑maneuver transfer, optimal for radius ratios $r_2/r_1<11.8$.
$$a_t=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}\qquad\text{transfer ellipse: perigee at }r_1,\ \text{apogee at }r_2$$
Every step below is just vis‑viva (Week 3) evaluated on the circle and on the transfer ellipse — the same recipe used for launch injection, GTO, and planetary departure.
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · derivation
Hohmann transfer — the equations
1
$v_c=\sqrt{\mu/r_1}\qquad v_p^{2}=2\mu\!\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1}-\dfrac{1}{r_1+r_2}\right)$
Circular speed on the inner orbit, and the perigee speed on the transfer ellipse.
2
$\Delta V_1=\sqrt{\dfrac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}}-1\right)$
First burn at perigee: speed up from the circle onto the transfer ellipse.
3
$\Delta V_2=\sqrt{\dfrac{\mu}{r_2}}\left(1-\sqrt{\dfrac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}}\right)$
Second burn at apogee: circularise onto the outer orbit.
4
$T_t=\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{a_t^{3}}{\mu}}=\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(r_1+r_2)^3}{8\mu}}$
Transfer time is half the ellipse period. Total cost $\Delta V=\Delta V_1+\Delta V_2$. Verify it live next.

Week 4 · Lecture 2 · coverage
Ground coverage — the footprint

Viewing angle → coverage circle

At height $h$ the payload's viewing angle is $2\alpha$ — the field of view of an imager or the beamwidth of an antenna. It projects a coverage circle on the ground of geocentric semi‑angle $\phi$ and diameter $D=2\phi R_E$.

Edge‑of‑coverage geometry

At the edge, elevation $\varepsilon$ and slant range $\rho$ follow from the same triangle as visibility: $\varepsilon=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\phi-\alpha$ and $\rho=R_E\,\dfrac{\sin\phi}{\sin(\pi/2-\varepsilon-\phi)}$.
$$D=2\phi R_E,\qquad \phi=-\varepsilon+\cos^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{R_E+h}\cos\varepsilon\Big)$$
Coverage is the performance side of the constellation trade: one satellite sees a cap of diameter $D$; global or continuous coverage needs many, phased in planes — which is where constellations come in.
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · coverage
Ground‑station visibility
  • A satellite is visible from any point inside a circle centred on the sub‑satellite point; the circle grows with altitude.
  • Signals near the horizon are weakened by the atmosphere, so useful coverage is limited to elevations above ~5°.
  • $\phi$ is the geocentric half‑angle of visibility; $s$ the slant range; $\varepsilon$ the elevation.
$$\phi=-\varepsilon+\cos^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{R_E+h}\cos\varepsilon\Big),\quad s=(R_E+h)\tfrac{\sin\phi}{\cos\varepsilon}$$

Pass duration

$\tau=2\phi/\omega_{ES}$
$\omega_{ES}^2=\omega_E^2+\omega^2-2\omega_E\omega\cos i$ is the satellite's angular rate relative to Earth. For LEO, using $\omega\approx631(R_E+h)^{-3/2}$ rad/s is within ~5–10%. A 500 km satellite is above 5° for ~9.2 min and above 10° for ~7.4 min on an overhead pass — most passes are shorter.

Week 4 · Lecture 2 · coverage
Repeating ground tracks & Earth‑synchronous orbits

Why tracks drift, then repeat

With the orbit fixed in inertial space and Earth turning underneath, successive passes cross the equator further west. The longitude shift per orbit has two parts: Earth's rotation during one nodal period, plus J₂ nodal regression.

The repeat condition

An Earth‑synchronous (repeating‑track) orbit closes its pattern when $n|\Delta\phi|=m\,2\pi$ — after $n$ orbits the track has slipped a whole number $m$ of turns and lands back on itself. Remote‑sensing satellites use this to revisit the same sites.
$$\Delta\phi_1=-2\pi\frac{\tau}{\tau_E},\qquad \Delta\phi_2=-\frac{3\pi J_2R_E^2\cos i}{a^2(1-e^2)^2},\qquad \Delta\phi=\Delta\phi_1+\Delta\phi_2$$
Sun‑synchronous orbits (L1) are the special case where the plane keeps pace with the Sun (~1°/day east); combined with a repeating track they give constant‑lighting, repeat‑revisit imaging — Landsat's design.

Week 4 · Lecture 2 · constellations
Constellation geometries — Walker & star patterns

Walker Delta pattern

Written $i\!:\!t/p/f$ — total sats $t$ in $p$ equally‑spaced planes, phasing integer $f$ ($0\le f\le p{-}1$). Each plane holds $s=t/p$ sats evenly spaced in true anomaly. A satellite at its node is phased by $f\cdot\text{PU}$ past the node of the next plane, where the Pattern Unit is $\text{PU}=360^{\circ}/t$.
e.g. $55^{\circ}\!:\!25/5/1\Rightarrow \text{PU}=14.4^{\circ}$

Star (polar) pattern

$n$ near‑polar planes with satellites evenly distributed. Adjacent co‑rotating planes are synchronised; the two counter‑rotating "seam" planes are not, which shapes coverage and nodal spacing. Used for global polar coverage (e.g. some comms systems).
Design reality: it is cheap to move a satellite within a plane (a small in‑plane phasing burn) but expensive to move it between planes (a plane change) — the L1 plane‑cost result. So constellations are launched plane‑by‑plane, and performance jumps at launches of 1, p, 2p… satellites. There are no universal rules — each design balances coverage, cost and performance.
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · power & thermal
Eclipse periods

Why it sizes the spacecraft

If solar arrays are the primary power source backed by batteries, the eclipse duration sets the battery size and the array's recharge margin. Computing the eclipse for a given orbit on any day of the year is a core mission‑analysis task.

The geometry

Treat Earth's shadow as a cylinder and Earth as a sphere. The satellite is eclipsed when the angle $\beta$ between the satellite direction and the anti‑Sun direction falls below a threshold set by $\sin\beta=R_E/r$. The Earth–Sun vector rotates through the year, so eclipse fraction varies seasonally.
$$\boldsymbol\rho=-\mathbf r_{\text{sun}},\qquad \beta=\sin^{-1}(R_E/r),\qquad \cos^{-1}(\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r_{\text{sat}})\le\beta\ \Rightarrow\ \text{eclipse}$$
In practice: estimate the Sun's ecliptic longitude for the day, sweep true anomaly to find shadow entry/exit, then convert those anomalies to a duration via Kepler's equation. Sun‑synchronous orbits can be chosen to minimise or even avoid eclipse.
Week 4 · Lecture 2 · timing
Launch windows
  • Launch window: the limited interval when a launch can meet the mission, set by dynamical and system constraints — the launch site must rotate through the target orbit plane.
  • Sun‑sync LEO: the node must align at a set angle to the Sun — a few minutes twice a day, often once a day after azimuth limits.
  • Rendezvous: geometric and temporal constraints; precise timing minimises phasing propellant.
  • GEO: system constraints (power, thermal, sun‑blinding) give two daily opportunities, around midday and midnight.
  • Interplanetary (Mars): planetary alignment sets the window — the synodic period recurs every ~780 days with a ~two‑week window; Earth's rotation narrows each daily opening to 30–60 min.
  • Mars must travel from $M_{\text{depart}}$ to $M_{\text{arrival}}$ while the spacecraft flies half the transfer (~259 days).
Launch windows tie the whole lecture together: the orbit you chose (L2), its plane precession (L1), coverage and eclipse all constrain when you can actually go.
