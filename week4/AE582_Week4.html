Week 4 · Lecture 1
The perturbed equation of motion
$$\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}\,\mathbf r=\mathbf b,\qquad \mathbf b=\mathbf a_{J_2}+\mathbf a_{\text{drag}}+\mathbf a_{\text{Sun}}+\mathbf a_{\text{Moon}}+\mathbf a_{\text{SRP}}+\dots$$

Osculating elements

Away from Earth the non‑uniform mass distribution makes the point‑mass force law invalid, and at low altitude the atmosphere adds drag. We keep the Keplerian description but let the "constants" $a,e,i,\Omega,\omega$ change slowly — the osculating elements that instantaneously match the true path.

Lagrange's planetary equations

The standard way to include perturbations is the method of variation of parameters, giving Lagrange's planetary equations for $\dot a,\dot e,\dot i,\dot\Omega,\dot\omega$. Note the caveat: these forms break down for $e=0$ or $i=0$ and need reformulation (equinoctial elements).
We can't solve this in closed form, so we ask how each perturbation moves the elements on average per orbit. The secular (non‑repeating) parts dominate mission design.
Week 4 · Lecture 1
Gravitational potential of the Earth
$$U(r,\phi,\lambda)=\frac{\mu}{r}\!\left[1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{r}\Big)^{\!n}\!J_nP_{n0}(\cos\phi)+\sum_{n,m}\!\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{r}\Big)^{\!n}\!(C_{nm}\cos m\lambda+S_{nm}\sin m\lambda)P_{nm}\right]$$
  • The convenient description of Earth's field is a spherical‑harmonic expansion.
  • Zonal terms $J_n$ reflect mass distribution independent of longitude; tesseral terms $C_{nm},S_{nm}$ capture longitude variation.
  • $J_2$ is three orders of magnitude larger than every other term — it dominates the gravitational perturbations.

The two secular rates from J₂
1
$U_{J_2}=\dfrac{\mu J_2 R_E^2}{2r^3}\big(3\sin^2\phi-1\big)$
Keep only the $n{=}2$ zonal term of the geopotential — the dominant disturbing potential.
2
$\sin\phi=\sin i\,\sin(\omega+\nu)$

3

Week 4 · Lecture 1
Regression of the line of nodes

Why the plane rotates

The equatorial bulge exerts a torque on the orbit's angular‑momentum vector. As the satellite crosses toward the equator it feels an extra pull; averaged over an orbit this makes the whole orbit plane rotate about Earth's spin axis — the ascending node slides along the equator.

Direction & size

For a prograde orbit ($i<90°$) the node moves west (regression); retrograde orbits advance east. The rate grows for low, low‑inclination orbits and is largest near the equator, zero at $i=90°$. Typical LEO: a few degrees per day.
$$\overline{\Omega}=\Omega_0-\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{J_2R_E^2}{p^2}\,\bar n\,t\,\cos i$$
This looks like a pure cost — but choose $i$ so the node keeps pace with Earth's motion around the Sun and it becomes the most useful orbit in remote sensing.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Turning drift into design — Sun‑synchronous orbits
$$\dot\Omega=+0.9856^{\circ}/\text{day}=\frac{360^{\circ}}{365.25\ \text{day}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos i=-\frac{\dot\Omega\,p^2}{1.5\,nJ_2R_E^2}$$

Lock the plane to the Sun

Choose $i$ so the node drifts exactly one turn per year, matching Earth's motion around the Sun. The orbit plane then keeps a fixed angle to the sunline — every pass over a given latitude happens at the same local solar time.

The workhorse orbit

Constant lighting is priceless for imaging and climate sensors (Landsat, Sentinel, most weather sats). It needs a slightly retrograde inclination, typically $i\approx96°\text{–}99°$ in LEO — the minus sign forces $i>90°$.
The next slide makes "locked" concrete: watch the local time at the node hold constant for a Sun‑sync orbit and drift for a fixed one.

Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Precession of the line of apsides & Molniya orbits

The apsides rotate within the plane

As the spacecraft crosses the equator it "sees" the extra pull of the bulge, which rotates the line of apsides (the perigee–apogee line) inside the orbit plane. Unlike a plane change this is an in‑plane rotation: perigee slowly marches around the orbit at the rate $\dot\omega$ from the J₂ derivation.

Freeze it at the critical inclination

Set $5\cos^2 i-1=0\Rightarrow i=63.4°$ (or $116.6°$). Then $\dot\omega=0$ and the apogee stops drifting. Choose any other inclination and the apogee slowly migrates between hemispheres — over a year it would spend part of its time over the wrong one, ruining a fixed‑apogee mission.
$$i_{\text{crit}}=\arccos\!\sqrt{\tfrac15}=63.435^{\circ}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \dot\omega=0$$

Molniya & Tundra

A highly eccentric $i=63.4°$ orbit parks apogee over the far north for hours per pass. This is how the USSR gave high‑latitude regions the coverage a geostationary belt over the equator physically cannot reach.

Who else uses it

Tundra orbits (24 h period, same inclination) serve regional broadcast; GPS sits in a nearby inclination band. The critical inclination is a genuine design constant, not a curiosity.
Nodal regression rotates the plane (out of plane); apsidal precession rotates the ellipse (in plane). J₂ drives both — and both can be turned from nuisance into design tool.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · special orbits
Molniya orbits — engineering the apogee dwell

The design recipe

  • Inclination $i=63.4°$ → $\dot\omega=0$: the apogee is frozen (L1).
  • Argument of perigee $\omega=270°$ → apogee sits at the orbit's northernmost point.
  • High eccentricity $e\approx0.74$ (600 km perigee, ~39 750 km apogee).
  • Period ~12 h → the ground track repeats, with two apogee loops per day.

Why the U.S.S.R. built them

A geostationary belt sits over the equator and barely clears the horizon from high latitudes, so it cannot serve the far north. By Kepler's 2nd law a Molniya satellite crawls near apogee, spending ~8 of its ~12 h hanging over northern latitudes. Three satellites phased 8 h apart give continuous high‑latitude coverage — communications, and later early‑warning.
"Molniya" means lightning. Tundra orbits use the same $63.4°$ inclination at a 24‑h period. See the ground track dwell over the U.S.S.R. on the next slide.

Week 4 · Lecture 1 · non‑conservative
Atmospheric drag
$$\mathbf F_D=\tfrac12\,\rho\,S C_D\,V_r^{2}\left(\frac{-\mathbf V_r}{|V_r|}\right),\qquad \frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau}\approx-3\pi\rho r\,(SC_D/M)$$

Strongest at perigee

For LEO spacecraft drag cannot be ignored. From the drag equation it is largest at perigee, where both density and speed peak. To first order that acts like an impulsive retro‑burn at perigee, lowering the semi‑major axis; in a circular orbit drag acts continuously and the change of period over one revolution is the formula above.

Contraction, then re‑entry

Drag removes energy ($\varepsilon=-\mu/2a$), so $a$ and $e$ shrink — the orbit contracts and circularises, then decays. Because Earth's atmosphere co‑rotates, the drag force also has an out‑of‑plane component, so it slowly changes inclination too. King‑Hele gives the radial/transverse/normal ($S,T,W$) components for a rotating atmosphere.
$V_r$ is velocity relative to the (rotating) atmosphere, $SC_D/M$ the ballistic coefficient, $\rho$ the local density. Solar activity swells $\rho$ and can shorten lifetimes dramatically.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · non‑conservative
Orbital lifetime
$$\tau\sim\frac{e_0^{2}}{2B}\left(1-\frac{11}{6}e_0+\frac{29}{16}e_0^{2}+\frac{7}{8}\frac{H}{a_0}\right),\qquad B\sim\sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{\mu}{a_0^3}\Big)}\,\tfrac{SC_D}{M}\rho_{p0}a_0e_0\,I_1\!\Big(\tfrac{a_0e_0}{H}\Big)e^{-e_0(1+a_0/H)}$$

From injection to re‑entry

Lifetime is the time from orbit injection until aerodynamic drag brings the vehicle back in. Here $e_0,a_0$ are the values when control ceases, $H$ the scale height near perigee, $\rho_{p0}$ the density there, and $I_1$ a modified Bessel function. The expression neglects atmospheric rotation.

Why it matters

Decay is slow high up (years at 600 km) but runs away low down (days near 300 km) because density rises exponentially. Uncontrolled re‑entries — Skylab over Australia, Cosmos 954B over Canada — show the hazard, and modern LEO design must plan a controlled de‑orbit or a <25‑year decay.
We modelled decay interactively in the Week 3 lifetime demo; here it is one perturbation among several, and the input to end‑of‑life planning we return to in Lecture 2.
Week 4 · Lecture 1
Third‑body & solar radiation pressure

Sun & Moon (third body)

Negligible in LEO but dominant at GEO, where they tilt the orbit plane ~0.85°/yr. This is why geostationary satellites spend most of their station‑keeping fuel on north–south (inclination) control — a theme we return to in Week 5.

Solar radiation pressure

Photons carry momentum ($\approx4.6\ \mu$Pa at 1 AU). For high area‑to‑mass craft it grows eccentricity and must be modelled — or harnessed, as in solar sails. Below ~0.005 m²/kg it is minor; above ~0.01 m²/kg it is significant.
Both are mostly periodic near Earth, but their long‑period terms matter for precise, long‑lived missions. For most LEO work: model J₂ + drag, treat these as corrections.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · case study
Precession as a free maneuver — TRACERS
1
The science fixes a Sun‑synchronous plane at a specific local time
TRACERS samples the northern magnetic cusp, which is best crossed from a Sun‑synchronous orbit at a 10:30 (or 02:30) local time of descending node (LTDN) — the plane's orientation to the Sun is a hard requirement.
2
Flying rideshare delivers the wrong local time — 06:00 LTDN
As a secondary payload (launched with PUNCH), TRACERS is dropped into whatever Sun‑sync plane the primary needs: a 06:00 LTDN orbit, several hours of local time away from the science orbit.
3
Rotating the plane directly is impossible
A 4.5‑hour LTDN shift is a ~67° rotation of the orbit plane (a RAAN change). At LEO speed, $\Delta V=2v\sin(\Delta\Omega/2)$ would need several km/s — far more propellant than a SMEX‑class spacecraft carries.
4
Instead: raise the altitude a little, keeping the inclination
A small in‑plane burn lifts the orbit (to ~610 km) while holding the ~590 km inclination. At the higher altitude the orbit is no longer perfectly Sun‑synchronous, so $\dot\Omega$ no longer matches the Sun and the plane drifts in local time.
5
Let it drift ~1 year, then drop back down
After about a year the LTDN has walked to the science value; lowering the altitude re‑establishes Sun‑synchronism at the new local time. Time — not propellant — bought the plane change.
This is the hinge of the week: the same $\dot\Omega$ we derived as a nuisance in L1 becomes the maneuver. The next slide puts numbers to it.
Week 4 · Lecture 1 · case study
TRACERS — the mission & the numbers

The drift logic

$$\dot\Omega=-\tfrac{3}{2}nJ_2\Big(\tfrac{R_E}{p}\Big)^{2}\cos i$$
Raising $a$ lowers $n$ and raises $p$, so $|\dot\Omega|$ drops below the $0.9856°/$day the Sun needs. The plane slips in local time at the difference rate; integrate it over ~1 year to cover the LTDN gap, then restore $a$ to re‑lock. A small in‑plane $\Delta V$ replaces a multi‑km/s plane change.

Week 4 · Lecture 2 of 2
L2
Mission analysis
The engineering discipline that turns objectives into a feasible, costed concept and its design drivers — objectives → drivers → concept → budgets → trade → baseline, developed live on Artemis I & II and a Death Star exercise. Orbital dynamics is used as input, not re‑derived.
What it is
Objectives in — concept & drivers out
Mission analysis is not detailed design. It is the feasibility‑level reasoning that takes objectives and constraints and returns a concept plus the few requirements — the design drivers — that dictate the vehicle.
What it is · when it happens
Early in the lifecycle — and iterative
Done in Pre‑Phase A / Phase A, long before the design is frozen. Mission concept, spacecraft design and cost form a loop — change one and the others move — and you circle it until the concept closes.
What it is · from objectives to requirements
Requirements flow down to a few drivers
Mission analysis turns objectives into traceable requirements. Of the hundreds that result, a handful — the design drivers — dictate the configuration. Trace every requirement back to an objective and forward to what it drives.
What it is · the key idea
A design driver cascades through the whole vehicle
A design driver is one of the few requirements that, once set, forces most of the configuration. Here "coverage/revisit" cascades into constellation size, launch, operations and spacecraft. Every mission has its own driver — the skill is finding it early.
What it is · the concept of operations
The Concept of Operations — the mission's story
A ConOps (or Design Reference Mission) is the day‑in‑the‑life storyline across every phase. It is what turns objectives into sizing: power per eclipse, data per pass, ΔV per maneuver, propellant for disposal.
Why it's done
Most of the cost is committed before it is spent
By the end of Phase A the concept has locked in ~70–80% of life‑cycle cost, though little is spent yet. Mission analysis is the one window where you can still change the outcome for the price of an engineer's time.
Why it's done

The mission-analysis process
The feedback loop matters as much as the boxes. The first baseline is rarely the last: tighten objectives, prune architectures, re‑run budgets, repeat.

A concept closes only when all budgets are satisfied at once. Mission analysis assembles the orbital‑dynamics results into this mission‑level balance — it does not re‑derive them.
How it's done · measuring “good”
Figures of merit — there is no single number
Architectures are judged on competing figures of merit — performance, cost, risk, schedule — which rarely peak together. Mission analysis weights them to the mission's priorities; the weighted utility is what the trade study ranks.
How it's done · margins & reserves
Never size to the exact number — carry margin
Every budget carries margin — typically ~5–10% on ΔV, plus mass, power and link reserves — to absorb launch dispersion, drag, aging and the unknowns of early design. A concept that closes only with zero margin hasn't really closed.
How it's done · the decision
The trade study
turning candidates into a baseline
1
Enumerate architectures
Orbit, single‑vs‑constellation, launch & transfer strategy, operations, disposal.
2
Score on figures of merit × weights
Performance, cost, risk, schedule — each rated 1–5, then weighted by how much the mission values it.
3
Rank & pick the baseline
The weighted winner is the baseline; the reasons it won are its design drivers.
4
Test sensitivity
Do the weights change the winner? A robust baseline wins across priorities; a fragile one needs another look.
There is no single "correct" architecture — only the best fit to the weighted objectives. Try it live next.

Case 1 · Artemis I
Artemis I
The objective, and the question we must answer

Objective

An uncrewed, integrated flight test of the SLS launch vehicle and the Orion crew vehicle, operating beyond low Earth orbit for the first time.

The mission-analysis question

What flight profile proves the system is ready to carry a crew — and what single requirement will that profile drive hardest? Let's reason to it.
We're not handed a trajectory. Mission analysis has to derive one from the objective. Next: from objective to driver.
Case 1 · Artemis I · developing the driver
Artemis I
From "test it beyond LEO" to the heat shield
1
List what the test must exercise
Ascent, trans‑lunar injection, deep‑space navigation & comms, power/thermal for weeks — and, uniquely, re‑entry from a lunar return.
2
Compare re‑entry speeds
Returning from the Moon, Orion meets the atmosphere at ~11 km/s, versus ~7.8 km/s from LEO. That difference is the crux.
3
Turn speed into heat load (the budget)
Energy scales as v²: (11/7.8)² ≈ 2× the energy to dissipate. Peak heating scales even more steeply (~v³): ≈ 2.8×. The thermal‑protection system sees conditions no LEO return ever produces.
4
Name the driver
→ The TPS (heat shield) is the design driver. The whole flight exists largely to validate it at true lunar‑return conditions before a crew rides behind it.
The move to internalise: an objective ("test beyond LEO") became a number (11 km/s) became a driver (TPS). No orbit was re‑derived — we spent the speed as a budget input.
Case 1 · Artemis I · concept, budgets & lesson
Artemis I
The profile the driver demands
  • Concept (reference mission): TLI → distant retrograde lunar orbit → trans‑Earth injection → high‑speed re‑entry.
  • Budgets that follow: ~26–42 day duration → consumables, power, thermal cycling; re‑entry ~11 km/s ≈ Mach 32 → the TPS budget; ~1.3 M miles of deep‑space comms & nav.
  • Secondary objectives: 13 CubeSats flown as rideshare — extra return for the same launch.
  • The lesson: the objective generated the architecture, and the architecture's hardest budget (TPS) is the driver. Analysis, not intuition, put the heat shield at the centre.
The whole chain closed with one number doing the driving. Now watch what happens when we put people on board.
Case 1 · Artemis I · the result
Artemis I
Did the analysis pay off?

The test flew the driver

Artemis I (uncrewed, 2022) flew the heat shield at true lunar‑return conditions — re‑entry at ~11 km/s, ~2,760 °C. It worked: NASA later confirmed that had crew been aboard, they would have been safe (the cabin held in the mid‑70s °F).

…and it caught what the models missed

Post‑flight inspection found unexpected char loss at 100+ locations — the Avcoat ablator cracked and shed more than predicted. A ~2‑year investigation traced it to Avcoat permeability trapping gases during the skip‑entry.
The payoff: because mission analysis flew the driving risk uncrewed first, NASA caught a crew‑critical issue — and fixed it with a trajectory change (a steeper, shorter re‑entry on Artemis II), not a costly heat‑shield rebuild. The test flight earned its keep.
Case 2 · Artemis II
Artemis II
Add a crew — and the driving figure of merit changes

Objective

The first crewed flight beyond LEO — a lunar fly‑by demonstrating the crewed system.

What changes

Humans on board make crew safety the dominant figure of merit. The analysis must guarantee a safe return even if a burn fails — reframing the whole trade around aborts.
Same lens, new priority. Let's develop how "guarantee a way home" turns into a specific trajectory choice.
Case 2 · Artemis II · developing the driver
Artemis II
From "keep the crew safe" to a free-return trajectory
1
Set the dominant figure of merit
With crew, safety / abort coverage outranks cost and performance. Every option is scored first on "how does the crew get home if X fails?"
2
Pose the failure question
If the trans‑lunar burn under‑performs, or a later burn fails, a powered return may be impossible. We need a path that returns the crew without a large burn.
3
Use geometry instead of propellant
A free‑return trajectory is aimed so lunar gravity itself slings Orion back onto an Earth‑intercept path — the Moon does the work, no rescue burn required.
4
Reconcile with the fly‑by geometry
A pure free‑return constrains lunar arrival. A hybrid free‑return keeps the protection through the critical early phase while allowing the desired geometry.
5
Add the hard constraint
Launch‑site latitude (Cape Canaveral, ~28.5°) fixes the reachable orbital plane — a driver the analysis designs around, not against.
The key idea: when safety drives, you buy it with trajectory design, not with more ΔV. Geometry is cheaper — and more reliable — than a rescue engine.
Case 2 · Artemis II · the trade & lesson
Artemis II
A concept chosen for safety, not for ΔV

The concept

Staged build‑up: perigee‑raise → apogee‑raise into a high Earth orbit for a full systems checkout → TLI onto a hybrid free‑return → lunar fly‑by → return.

The trade made explicit

This profile is not minimum‑ΔV. It costs extra maneuvers and constrains timing — and wins anyway, because the weighted figure of merit is abort safety. Risk is ranked above propellant.
Lesson: Artemis I and II fly similar hardware, yet mission analysis chose different architectures because the driver changed — test‑validation (TPS) vs crew safety (free‑return). Same method; different winning figure of merit.
Case 2 · Artemis II · the result
Artemis II
The safety-driven design delivered

It flew — April 1–11, 2026

Wiseman, Glover, Koch & Hansen — the first crew beyond LEO since 1972 — looped the Moon on a free‑return and came home safely, reaching 252,756 miles from Earth, a new human distance record.

The mission‑analysis calls that worked

The free‑return (chosen for abort safety) needed only minor corrections — lunar gravity brought them home. Orion's initial perigee was deliberately left suborbital as a safe‑abort measure. And re‑entry was flown steeper — the Artemis I lesson — to protect the heat shield.
The lesson realised: every driver the analysis named — safety via free‑return, the trajectory tweak from Artemis I — became a real decision that kept the crew safe. Mission analysis wasn't paperwork; it flew.
Case 5 · in-class exercise · the Death Star
The Death Star
Now you run the analysis

The Emperor's brief (objectives)

  • Project decisive military power across the galaxy.
  • Be able to destroy a planet.
  • Rule through fear — an unmistakable deterrent.
  • Constraints: vast but finite resources; a build schedule; must survive a rebellion.

Your job (with the class)

Don't jump to "a Death Star." First reason from the objectives: what do they actually demand of the design? Then propose the Emperor's priorities and let the trade pick the baseline.
This is the whole method turned into a decision you make: objectives → drivers → candidate concepts → trade → baseline. Let's develop the drivers first.
Case 5 · the Death Star · developing the drivers
The Death Star
From the objectives to the central tension
1
Decompose the objective into needs
Firepower (destroy a planet), reach (galaxy‑wide mobility), fear (a visible, awe‑inspiring deterrent), and it must survive a determined enemy.
2
Spot the tension
Firepower + fear pull toward one overwhelming station (a monolith). Survivability + cost + schedule pull toward a distributed force. The two pull in opposite directions.
3
Name the candidate architectures
One Death Star · a Star Destroyer fleet · a planet‑based superlaser · a smaller mobile battle station. Each is best on a different objective.
4
There is no obvious answer
The winner depends entirely on which objective the Emperor weights most. So — set the priorities and run the trade.
This is the moment to poll the class: which objective matters most? Then test it live on the next slide.
Case 5 · the Death Star · the operational orbit
The Death Star
Selecting the operational orbit
1
Orbit is a decision too
To destroy a planet and loom over it as a threat, the station must enter orbit around the target. Which orbit? That is a mission‑analysis choice, exactly like an Earth satellite's.
2
The orbit options
Low — max weapon effect & intimidation, but exposed to surface defences and starfighters. Synchronous — safer standoff, dominates one hemisphere. Distant standoff — safest, but a weak deterrent.
3
The objective points low

Case 5 · the Death Star · the baseline & the lesson
The Death Star
What the Empire chose — and the risk it ignored

The Empire's choice

It weighted firepower & fear above all → the monolith: one Death Star. In the trade that is the correct baseline for those weights.

The risk the weights hid

Look at the same row: the monolith scores lowest on survivability — a single point of failure. A disciplined trade flags that as the #1 risk. A two‑metre exhaust port then turned that risk into catastrophe.
The lesson: weight the hardest / riskiest element, not the flashiest capability. A trade study that under‑weights risk picks a baseline that looks unstoppable but fails where it is weakest — here, one exhaust port on an otherwise invincible station.

Lecture 2 — Mission analysis

  • Objectives → concept + design drivers; requirements flow down.
  • Size with budgets and margins; rank with figures of merit.
  • Artemis I: fly the driver first → caught the heat‑shield issue.
  • Artemis II & Death Star: weight safety/risk, not the flashiest capability.
One line: a real orbit drifts under perturbations you can model or exploit; mission analysis then picks the orbit a mission needs and prices — in ΔV, coverage, power, risk and timing — the cost of using it.
