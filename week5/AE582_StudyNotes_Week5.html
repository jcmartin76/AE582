Propulsion Systems — Study Guide

Chemical & electric rocket propulsion for spacecraft
AE 582 Satellite Engineering (graduate) · companion to Week 5 Lecture 2 · after TU Delft §1.1, §1.3 and MIT 16.50. Interactive: use the contents to navigate, expand derivations, and try the embedded simulators.

1 · Overview & figures of merit

A spacecraft propulsion system solves two coupled problems. The mass problem — how much propellant a mission's velocity budget costs — is governed by the rocket equation. The gas-dynamics problem — how a hot, high-pressure gas is turned into a fast, directed jet — is governed by ideal rocket theory and the de Laval nozzle. These notes develop both, following TU Delft Rocket & Onboard Propulsion (§1.1, §1.3) and MIT 16.50, and extend them to the satellite-relevant topics: staging, mission budgets, and electric propulsion.

A propulsion taxonomy

Every rocket obeys the same rocket equation, but they differ in where the energy comes from relative to the propellant:

ClassEnergy source$I_{sp}$ (s)ThrustTypical use
Chemical — solidpropellant is fuel and energy~250very highboosters
Chemical — liquid (kerolox)propellant is fuel and energy~350highlaunch stages
Chemical — liquid (hydrolox)propellant is fuel and energy~450highupper stages
Electrothermal (resistojet/arcjet)electric → heat300–1000lowstation-keeping
Electrostatic (ion/Hall)electric → kinetic1500–4000mNorbit raising, deep space

In a chemical rocket the propellant carries its own energy, so the achievable jet velocity is capped by the combustion temperature. In a non-chemical rocket the energy and propellant are separate, so the jet velocity is limited only by how much power (and power-plant mass) you are willing to carry — a distinction that dominates satellite propulsion design (§15).

Figures of merit

Two numbers characterise any thruster: thrust $F_T$ (how hard it pushes) and specific impulse $I_{sp}$ (how efficiently it uses propellant). A third, thrust-to-weight $F_T/(m\,g_0)$, decides whether a vehicle can leave the pad. The rest of these notes is, in essence, the derivation and interplay of these three.

2 · Thrust & the momentum theorem

Thrust is the reaction to expelling mass. It is obtained from the steady-flow momentum theorem applied to a control volume (CV) that encloses the engine.

The net axial force equals the rate of momentum outflow plus the net pressure force on the CV boundary. With ambient pressure $p_a$ acting everywhere except over the exit plane (area $A_e$, pressure $p_e$), and negligible inlet momentum:

Thrust equation
$$F_T=\underbrace{\dot m\,v_e}_{\text{momentum}}+\underbrace{(p_e-p_a)\,A_e}_{\text{pressure}}=\dot m\,v_{eq},\qquad v_{eq}=v_e+\frac{(p_e-p_a)A_e}{\dot m}$$

The momentum term $\dot m v_e$ carries most of the thrust; the pressure term is the exit/ambient mismatch. Rolling both into the equivalent (effective) jet velocity $v_{eq}$ gives the compact form $F_T=\dot m\,v_{eq}$. When the nozzle is perfectly expanded ($p_e=p_a$), $v_{eq}=v_e$ and thrust is simply $\dot m v_e$; MIT writes this idealised case as $F=\dot m\,c$ with $c$ the far-field effective exhaust velocity.

Because $p_a$ falls with altitude, the pressure term grows and thrust rises monotonically to a vacuum maximum. This is not a small effect for large first-stage engines: a sea-level-to-vacuum thrust rise of 15–25% is typical.

Vary mass flow, exhaust velocity, exit pressure/area and altitude; watch the momentum and pressure contributions and the climb to the vacuum asymptote.

Q. Why can the pressure term be negative at sea level for a vacuum-optimised nozzle?
A large area ratio drives $p_e$ well below sea-level $p_a$, so $(p_e-p_a)<0$ and the pressure term subtracts from thrust — the nozzle is over-expanded (§12). Such a nozzle only pays off at altitude.

3 · Specific impulse & efficiencies

Specific impulse measures how much impulse you extract per unit of propellant consumed.

Specific impulse
$$I_{sp}=\frac{1}{g_0}\,\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{t_b}F_T\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_0^{t_b}\dot m\,dt}\;\xrightarrow[\;v_{eq}=\text{const}\;]{}\;\frac{v_{eq}}{g_0}=\frac{c}{g_0}$$

It is total impulse per unit propellant weight, hence units of seconds. The constant $g_0=9.80665~\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is a defined reference, not local gravity, so $I_{sp}$ is a property of the engine, independent of where it flies. The effective exhaust velocity $c\equiv v_{eq}=I_{sp}g_0$ is the same quantity in velocity units.

Energy efficiencies

Three efficiencies connect the chemical/electrical energy input to useful thrust power:

EfficiencyDefinitionMeaning
Thermal $\eta_{th}$jet KE / energy input $=\tfrac12\dot m c^2/\dot E$how well energy becomes jet kinetic energy
Propulsive $\eta_{p}$$\dfrac{2\,u/c}{1+(u/c)^2}$how well jet KE becomes vehicle work at flight speed $u$
Overall $\eta_{o}$$\eta_{th}\,\eta_{p}$thrust power / energy input

Propulsive efficiency peaks when the jet speed matches the flight speed ($u=c$) — the physical reason air-breathing engines (small $c$) are efficient at aircraft speeds while rockets (huge $c$) are not, but rockets do not care because they carry no air and must work in vacuum.

4 · The rocket equation

The rocket equation converts a required velocity change into a propellant mass — and reveals why reaching orbit is so unforgiving.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation
$$\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln\frac{M_0}{M_f}=v_{eq}\ln\Lambda,\qquad \Lambda\equiv\frac{M_0}{M_f}=e^{\Delta V/v_{eq}}$$
Show derivation

In free space thrust is the only force on the vehicle of instantaneous mass $M$:

$$M\frac{dv}{dt}=-v_{eq}\frac{dM}{dt}\;(dM<0)\;\Rightarrow\;dv=-v_{eq}\frac{dM}{M}.$$

Integrating from wet mass $M_0$ to dry mass $M_f$ with constant $v_{eq}$ gives $\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln(M_0/M_f)$. If $v_{eq}=v_{eq}(t)$ only the integral form survives.

Because $\Delta V$ sits in the exponent, the mass ratio grows exponentially: doubling $\Delta V$ squares $\Lambda$. Writing $M_0=M_s+M_p+M_{pl}$ (structure, propellant, payload) and defining the structural coefficient $\varepsilon_s=M_s/(M_s+M_p)$, the achievable payload fraction is

$$\pi=\frac{M_{pl}}{M_0}=\frac{1/\Lambda-\varepsilon_s}{1-\varepsilon_s}.$$

Payload vanishes when $1/\Lambda=\varepsilon_s$, i.e. at the single-stage ceiling $\Delta V_{\max}=v_{eq}\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)$. For $\varepsilon_s\approx0.1$, $v_{eq}\approx3.4$ km/s this is only $\approx7.8$ km/s — below the $\approx9.4$ km/s needed for LEO. Hence staging (§6).

Q. A stage has $v_{eq}=3.0$ km/s and must deliver 4.5 km/s at $\varepsilon_s=0.08$. Is a single stage feasible?
$\Lambda=e^{4.5/3.0}=4.48$, so $1/\Lambda=0.223>\varepsilon_s$. Payload fraction $\pi=(0.223-0.08)/0.92=0.156$ — feasible, with 16% payload.

5 · Gravity loss & optimum acceleration

On a real launch, gravity subtracts speed throughout the burn. There is an optimum thrust level that balances this loss against the mass penalty of a bigger engine — a classic MIT 16.50 result.

For a vertical launch, adding the gravitational term to the momentum balance and integrating (constant $c,g$):

Rocket equation with gravity loss
$$v-v_0=c\,\ln\frac{M_0}{M(t)}-\underbrace{g\,t}_{\text{gravity loss}}$$

The gravity loss $g\,t$ shrinks if the burn is shorter, i.e. if thrust (hence initial acceleration $a_0=F/M_0$) is larger. Define the thrust-to-initial-weight ratio $n=a_0/g$. So why not make $n$ huge? Because engine mass scales with thrust: with $\delta=$ (engine weight)/thrust, the engine mass fraction is $n\delta$, which eats into payload. The two effects pull oppositely.

Show the optimum-acceleration analysis (MIT Lec 1)

With constant $\dot m$, burn time to any mass $M$ is $t=(M_0-M)/\dot m=(1-M/M_0)\,c/(n g)$, so the gravity loss is $g t=(c/n)(1-M/M_0)$. Splitting the burnout mass into fractions $m'_{pay}+m'_{str}+n\delta$ (payload, structure, engine$\,\propto n$), the attained increment is

$$\frac{v_b-v_0}{c}=-\ln\!\big(m'_{pay}+m'_{str}+n\delta\big)-\frac{1-m'_{pay}-m'_{str}-n\delta}{n}.$$

The log term (ideal $\Delta V$) worsens as $n$ grows (heavier engine); the last term (gravity loss) improves as $n$ grows (shorter burn). Setting $\partial/\partial n=0$ yields a quadratic whose positive root is $n_{opt}$. For representative values $\varepsilon_s\approx0.1,\ \delta\approx0.02$, the optimum sits in

$$\boxed{\,n_{opt}=a_0/g\approx 3\text{ to }4\,}$$

— the "3–4 g" launch acceleration familiar to astronauts. Including drag (which penalises high speed low in the atmosphere) pushes the optimum slightly lower for small vehicles.

The takeaway: launch vehicles are sized near $a_0/g\approx3$–4 not by comfort but by a genuine optimum between gravity loss and engine mass. Drag and steering add a further $\sim$0.3–0.5 km/s, and together the losses turn $\sim$7.8 km/s of orbital speed into the $\sim$9.4 km/s LEO budget.

6 · Staging

Staging defeats the exponential by discarding empty structure mid-flight. Because $\Delta V$ adds across stages while mass ratios multiply, several modest ratios achieve what one impossible ratio cannot.

Multi-stage ΔV
$$\Delta V_{total}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} v_{eq,i}\,\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{s,i}+(1-\varepsilon_{s,i})\lambda_i}$$

Each stage's "payload" is the entire stack above it, so the ratios compound. Maximising payload for a fixed $\Delta V_{total}$ is a constrained optimisation (Lagrange multipliers); for identical stages the optimum is an equal split of $\Delta V$. Returns diminish quickly — each added stage buys less margin at rising structural, separation and integration cost — so practical launchers use two or three stages.

Note how a single stage gives a negative payload fraction for a 9.4 km/s budget, while two or three stages recover it.

7 · Mission ΔV budgets

For satellites, the rocket equation is applied to a ΔV budget — the sum of every manoeuvre from launch to disposal. Sizing the propellant is then a single application of Tsiolkovsky.

ManoeuvreTypical ΔVNotes
Launch to LEO~9.4 km/sorbital speed + gravity/drag losses
LEO → GTO (Hohmann)~2.4 km/sperigee burn
GTO → GEO~1.5 km/sapogee burn + plane change
GEO station-keeping~50 m/s/yrN–S dominates (luni-solar)
De-orbit / graveyard~5–150 m/send-of-life disposal

Two design levers follow directly. High $I_{sp}$ shrinks propellant mass exponentially — decisive for the large, repeated $\Delta V$ of orbit raising and years of station-keeping, which is why electric propulsion (§15) has taken over these tasks. Splitting the budget between a chemical apogee kick (high thrust, fast) and electric station-keeping (high $I_{sp}$, patient) is common practice.

Q. Why is electric propulsion attractive for station-keeping but not for launch?
Station-keeping needs small, repeated $\Delta V$ over years, where the exponential propellant saving of high $I_{sp}$ dominates and low thrust is acceptable. Launch needs $a_0/g>1$ immediately (§5), which mN-class electric thrust cannot provide.

8 · Ideal rocket theory

Ideal rocket theory predicts the jet velocity $v_e$, mass flow $\dot m$ and exit pressure $p_e$ from the chamber state $(p_C,T_C)$ and gas properties alone, using a simplified geometry and a set of assumptions.

The nine assumptions

Idealisations
  1. Perfect gas, $p=\rho(R_A/M_W)T$.
  2. Calorically ideal: $c_p,c_v,\gamma$ constant.
  3. Constant, homogeneous composition.
  4. Steady flow.
  5. Isentropic flow (adiabatic + reversible).
  6. One-dimensional (axial variation only).
  7. Purely axial velocity.
  8. No external body forces.
  9. Negligible chamber velocity, $v_c\approx0$.

Plus two data assumptions: the chamber state $(p_C,T_C)$ and properties $(\gamma,c_p,M_W)$ are known and constant.

Governing equations

$$\begin{aligned} \text{continuity:}\quad &\rho v A=\dot m=\text{const} & \text{momentum (Euler):}\quad & dp=-\rho v\,dv\\ \text{energy:}\quad & h+\tfrac12 v^2=c_pT_C & \text{state, sound, Mach:}\quad & p=\rho\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T,\ a^2=\gamma\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T,\ M=\tfrac{v}{a} \end{aligned}$$

closed by the isentropic relations, from which everything else is algebra:

$$\frac{T_C}{T}=1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2,\qquad \frac{p_C}{p}=\left(\frac{T_C}{T}\right)^{\!\gamma/(\gamma-1)},\qquad \frac{\rho_C}{\rho}=\left(\frac{T_C}{T}\right)^{\!1/(\gamma-1)}.$$

9 · Nozzle flow & the area–Mach relation

Why must a rocket nozzle first converge, then diverge? The answer is one line of gas dynamics.

Area–velocity relation
$$\frac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\,\frac{dv}{v}$$
Show derivation

Log-differentiate continuity: $\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho}+\dfrac{dv}{v}+\dfrac{dA}{A}=0$. From Euler $dp=-\rho v\,dv$ and $a^2=dp/d\rho$, $\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho}=-M^2\dfrac{dv}{v}$. Eliminating $d\rho/\rho$ gives the boxed result.

For $M<1$ the factor $(M^2-1)$ is negative, so accelerating the flow ($dv>0$) needs a contracting area; for $M>1$ it needs an expanding area. The flow can only cross $M=1$ where $dA=0$ — the throat. Hence the convergent–divergent (de Laval) shape.

The area–Mach relation

Combining continuity at the throat with the isentropic relations gives the exit Mach number as a function of area ratio alone:

$$\frac{A}{A^*}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}.$$

On the supersonic branch this is monotonic, so a chosen $\varepsilon=A_e/A^*$ selects a unique $M_e$, hence unique $p_e/p_C$ and $T_e/T_C$. This is the precise sense in which geometry fixes the exit pressure.

Q. Each $A/A^*>1$ corresponds to two Mach numbers. Which does a rocket realise, and why?
Both a subsonic and a supersonic solution satisfy the area–Mach relation. A rocket runs the supersonic branch in the diverging section because the throat is choked at $M=1$ and the flow continues to accelerate ($dv>0$ with $dA>0$ requires $M>1$).

10 · Jet velocity

How fast can the jet go? Energy conservation plus the isentropic relations give a closed form.

Jet velocity
$$v_e=\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\,\frac{R_A}{M_W}\,T_C\left[\,1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_C}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}$$
Show derivation

Energy with $v_c\approx0$: $\tfrac12 v_e^2=c_p(T_C-T_e)\Rightarrow v_e=\sqrt{2c_p(T_C-T_e)}$. Insert $T_e/T_C=(p_e/p_C)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ and $c_p=\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}$ to obtain the boxed form.

Three levers raise exhaust speed: hotter chamber ($v_e\propto\sqrt{T_C}$), lighter molecules ($v_e\propto1/\sqrt{M_W}$ — the strongest lever, and why hydrogen wins), and deeper expansion (smaller $p_e/p_C$, with diminishing returns). The thermodynamic ceiling, at $p_e/p_C\to0$, is $v_{e,\max}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T_C}$.

Compare hydrolox, kerolox and solid presets; note how $M_W$ moves the whole curve.

11 · Choked mass flow

The throat sets the mass flow. Once it is sonic, the flow is choked — the flow rate is fixed by the chamber and cannot be increased by lowering the back pressure. This is how a rocket regulates its own mass flow.

Choked mass flow
$$\dot m=\frac{p_C\,A^*}{\sqrt{\frac{R_A}{M_W}T_C}}\,\Gamma(\gamma),\qquad \Gamma(\gamma)=\sqrt{\gamma\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}}$$
Show derivation

Evaluate $\dot m=\rho^*v^*A^*$ at the throat, where $M=1$ so $v^*=a^*=\sqrt{\gamma(R_A/M_W)T^*}$. Use the sonic isentropic ratios $T^*/T_C=\tfrac{2}{\gamma+1}$ and $\rho^*/\rho_C=(\tfrac{2}{\gamma+1})^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ with $\rho_C=p_C/[(R_A/M_W)T_C]$. The $\gamma$-factors collect into the Vandenkerckhove function $\Gamma(\gamma)$.

$\dot m$ rises with chamber pressure $p_C$ and throat area $A^*$, and — note the sign clash with $v_e$ — with high $M_W$ and low $T_C$. Thrust $\dot m v_{eq}$ is the product, so a real design balances these opposing pulls. Practically, mass flow is set at design by $A^*$ and controlled in flight by $p_C$ (throttling the feed).

12 · Nozzle performance: $C_F$ & $c^*$

Two coefficients factor engine performance cleanly: $c^*$ isolates the chamber and propellant, $C_F$ isolates the nozzle. Their product is the effective exhaust velocity.

Performance factoring
$$c^*=\frac{p_C A^*}{\dot m}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\sqrt{\frac{R_A}{M_W}T_C},\qquad C_F=\frac{F_T}{p_C A^*},\qquad v_{eq}=C_F\,c^*,\quad I_{sp}=\frac{C_F c^*}{g_0}$$

$c^*$ (characteristic velocity) measures the energy release — chamber temperature and molecular mass — and is a clean diagnostic of combustion quality. $C_F$ (thrust coefficient) measures how well the nozzle converts chamber pressure into thrust. The ideal thrust coefficient is

$$C_F=\Gamma(\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left[1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_C}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}+\varepsilon\,\frac{p_e-p_a}{p_C}.$$

The pressure term shows that, for a fixed geometry, $C_F$ (and thrust) is maximised when $p_e=p_a$ — the nozzle is adapted. Since $p_e$ is fixed by $\varepsilon$ but $p_a$ varies, a fixed nozzle is adapted at only one altitude; larger area ratios help at altitude but over-expand (and may separate) low down.

Q. Increasing $\varepsilon$ always lowers $p_e$. Why not use an enormous area ratio on a first stage?
Low down, a very small $p_e$ makes the nozzle badly over-expanded; the pressure term $\varepsilon(p_e-p_a)/p_C$ goes strongly negative and the boundary layer can separate, causing side loads. First stages use modest $\varepsilon$ (or altitude-compensating nozzles); upper stages use large $\varepsilon$.

13 · Nozzle types & real-flow losses

Real nozzles trade ideal performance for mass, length and manufacturability, and real flow deviates from the isentropic ideal.

Nozzle geometries

  • Conical — simple, but the divergent flow is non-axial. A half-angle $\alpha$ costs a divergence loss $\lambda=\tfrac12(1+\cos\alpha)$ on the momentum term (e.g. $\lambda\approx0.983$ at $15^\circ$).
  • Bell (contoured) — turns the flow back toward axial, recovering most of the divergence loss in a shorter length; the standard for launch engines.
  • Annular / aerospike — the flow expands against an outer free boundary set by ambient pressure, giving altitude compensation (near-adapted over a range of $p_a$) at the cost of complexity and cooling.

Deviations from ideal theory

Shortfalls are collected into efficiencies on $c^*$ (combustion) and $C_F$ (nozzle), each typically 0.90–0.99:

  • Finite-rate chemistry — real expansion lies between the frozen and shifting-equilibrium limits (§14), changing $T_e$, composition and $v_e$.
  • Real-gas / variable properties — $\gamma,c_p,M_W$ drift with temperature and composition along the nozzle.
  • Friction & heat loss — boundary layers and wall cooling make the flow non-isentropic and non-adiabatic.
  • Two-phase flow — condensed particles (Al$_2$O$_3$ in metallised solids) lag the gas thermally and in velocity.

14 · Chemical propulsion

In a chemical rocket the propellant is the energy source, so performance is capped by the combustion temperature and the exhaust molecular mass — the two levers in $v_e\propto\sqrt{T_C/M_W}$.

Liquid vs. solid

Liquid bipropellantSolid
$I_{sp}$higher (350–465 s)lower (~250 s)
Throttle / restartyesno (burns to completion)
Complexityhigh (turbopumps, plumbing)low (cast grain)
Readiness / storageoften cryogenicstorable, instant

Solid motors regulate thrust through the burning-area geometry of the grain (progressive, neutral or regressive), since $\dot m\propto$ burn area $\times$ regression rate and the regression rate rises with chamber pressure. Liquid engines regulate through feed pressure and are chosen for the controllability needed by upper stages and landers.

Reacting gases: frozen vs. equilibrium

The assumption of constant composition (§8) is an idealisation. As the gas cools through the nozzle, recombination reactions (e.g. $\mathrm{H+OH\rightarrow H_2O}$) release energy and change $M_W$ and $\gamma$. Two limits bound reality:

  • Frozen flow — composition fixed at the chamber value; no recombination energy recovered (a lower bound on $v_e$).
  • Shifting (equilibrium) flow — composition re-equilibrates instantly at every station; maximum recombination energy recovered (an upper bound).

Real nozzles fall between, nearer equilibrium in the throat and toward frozen far downstream where densities are low and reaction rates slow. This is why the effective $\gamma$ and $M_W$ used in the ideal formulas are flow-averaged values, not simple chamber values.

15 · Electric & advanced propulsion

When energy and propellant are separate, there is no combustion-temperature ceiling on $v_e$. But higher $v_e$ demands more power — and power-plant mass — so there is an optimum exhaust velocity, a central MIT 16.50 result and the key to satellite electric propulsion.

A taxonomy

  • Electrothermal (resistojet, arcjet) — electricity heats the propellant, then a nozzle expands it. Modest $I_{sp}$ (300–1000 s).
  • Electrostatic (gridded ion, Hall) — ions are accelerated in an electric field to very high $v_e$ (1500–4000 s $I_{sp}$).
  • Electromagnetic (MPD, PPT) — Lorentz force accelerates a plasma.

Optimum exhaust velocity (power-limited)

For an electric system the total mass includes the power plant. With jet power $P=\tfrac12\dot m c^2=\tfrac12 F c$, a specific power-plant mass $\alpha_e$ (kg/W) and initial acceleration $a_0=F/m_{tot}$, the payload fraction is

Power-limited payload fraction
$$\frac{m_{pay}}{m_{tot}}=e^{-\Delta V/c}-\frac{\alpha_e\,a_0}{2}\,c$$

The first term rewards high $c$ (less propellant); the second penalises it (heavier power plant, $m_{elect}=\alpha_e P\propto c$). There is therefore a finite optimum:

$$\frac{\Delta V}{c^2}\,e^{-\Delta V/c}=\frac{\alpha_e a_0}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{opt}\ \text{finite}.$$
Show the optimisation & a worked estimate (MIT Lec 5)

Differentiate the payload fraction with respect to $c$ and set to zero: $\dfrac{\Delta V}{c^2}e^{-\Delta V/c}=\dfrac{\alpha_e a_0}{2}$. Eliminating the technology group between this and the payload expression gives the design curve $\dfrac{m_{pay}}{m_{tot}}=e^{-\Delta V/c}\big(1-\tfrac{\Delta V}{c}\big)$, which only makes sense for $\Delta V/c<1$.

Estimate: take $\Delta V=10$ km/s and $\alpha_e=20$ kg/kW $=0.02$ kg/W. Insisting on $m_{pay}/m_{tot}=0.5$ gives $(\Delta V/c)_{opt}\approx0.3$ (so $c\approx33$ km/s, $I_{sp}\approx3400$ s) and $a_0\approx7\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\approx7\times10^{-5}\,g$. The burn time is then

$$t=\frac{m_{prop}}{\dot m}=\frac{c}{a_0}\left(1-e^{-\Delta V/c}\right)\approx1.2\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{s}\approx 140\ \text{days}.$$

Electric propulsion trades time for mass: the same mission by a chemical stage would leave a payload fraction of order 1%, but takes days, not months.

The practical message for satellites: unlike chemical rockets, where you always want the highest $v_e$ you can get, electric systems have a mission-specific optimum $I_{sp}$ set by the required $\Delta V$, the allowed transit time (through $a_0$), and the power-plant technology $\alpha_e$.

17 · Worked examples

Worked example 1 — sizing a vacuum nozzle

Given: $p_C=70$ bar, $T_C=3500$ K, $\gamma=1.20$, $M_W=22$ kg/kmol, $\varepsilon=40$, $A^*=0.02~\mathrm{m^2}$.

$R_A/M_W=8314/22=377.9~\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$; $\ \Gamma(1.2)=\sqrt{1.2(2/2.2)^{11}}=0.6485$.

$c^*=\sqrt{377.9\times3500}/0.6485=1773~\mathrm{m/s}$; $\ \dot m=p_CA^*/c^*=(7\times10^6)(0.02)/1773=79.0~\mathrm{kg/s}$.

Area–Mach at $\varepsilon=40\Rightarrow M_e=4.24$, so $p_e/p_C=(1+0.1M_e^2)^{-6}=2.1\times10^{-3}$, $p_e=14.6$ kPa, $T_e=1251$ K.

$v_e=3193~\mathrm{m/s}$, $A_e=0.80~\mathrm{m^2}$. In vacuum $F_T=\dot m v_e+p_eA_e=\mathbf{263.8~kN}$, $C_F=F_T/(p_CA^*)=\mathbf{1.884}$, $I_{sp}=C_Fc^*/g_0=\mathbf{340.7~s}$. (At sea level the same nozzle is heavily over-expanded: $F_T=182.7$ kN, $I_{sp}=236$ s — an upper-stage design.)

Worked example 2 — electric vs chemical for a 6 km/s transfer

Chemical (hydrolox, $c=4.4$ km/s, $\varepsilon_s=0.1$): $\Lambda=e^{6/4.4}=3.90$, payload fraction $\pi=(1/3.90-0.1)/0.9=0.174$ — 17%, delivered in hours.

Electric ($c=30$ km/s, $I_{sp}\approx3060$ s): propellant fraction $=1-e^{-6/30}=0.181$, so only 18% propellant vs 74% for chemical — but the power plant mass and a transit of weeks must be carried. The choice is set by whether the mission can spend time (§15).

18 · Self-check

Check your understanding — reveal each answer after attempting it.

1. Two engines have the same thrust but $I_{sp}$ of 300 s and 450 s. For a fixed $\Delta V$, how do their propellant masses compare?
Propellant mass $\propto(1-e^{-\Delta V/(I_{sp}g_0)})$. The higher-$I_{sp}$ engine needs exponentially less propellant; the gap widens as $\Delta V$ grows.
2. Why is the throat, not the exit, the station that fixes $\dot m$?
Because the throat chokes at $M=1$: for given chamber conditions there is exactly one mass flow that makes the throat sonic, and downstream conditions cannot change it.
3. A nozzle is adapted at 10 km altitude. Is it over- or under-expanded at sea level? In vacuum?
Sea level ($p_a$ higher than design): over-expanded ($p_eunder-expanded.
4. For electric propulsion, why is 'as high as possible' the wrong target for $I_{sp}$?
Higher $c$ means more jet power ($P=\tfrac12Fc$) and thus more power-plant mass ($\propto\alpha_e c$). Past the optimum, the added power-plant mass outweighs the propellant saving and payload falls.
5. Launch vehicles are sized near $a_0/g\approx3$–4. What two competing effects set this?
Gravity loss $\propto g t$ falls with higher thrust (shorter burn), but engine mass rises with thrust. The optimum of $\Delta V$ against these gives $n_{opt}\approx3$–4.

19 · Formula sheet

Core relations
Thrust$F_T=\dot m v_e+(p_e-p_a)A_e=\dot m v_{eq}=C_F p_C A^*$
Specific impulse$I_{sp}=v_{eq}/g_0=C_F c^*/g_0$
Rocket equation$\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln\Lambda,\quad \Lambda=M_0/M_f=e^{\Delta V/v_{eq}}$
Gravity loss (vertical)$v-v_0=c\ln(M_0/M)-g t;\quad n_{opt}=a_0/g\approx3\text{–}4$
Payload fraction$\pi=(1/\Lambda-\varepsilon_s)/(1-\varepsilon_s)$
Area–velocity$dA/A=(M^2-1)\,dv/v$
Area–Mach$A/A^*=\tfrac1M[\tfrac{2}{\gamma+1}(1+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2)]^{(\gamma+1)/2(\gamma-1)}$
Jet velocity$v_e=\sqrt{\tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T_C[1-(p_e/p_C)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}]}$
Choked mass flow$\dot m=\dfrac{p_CA^*}{\sqrt{(R_A/M_W)T_C}}\Gamma,\ \ \Gamma=\sqrt{\gamma(\tfrac{2}{\gamma+1})^{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)}}$
Coefficients$c^*=\sqrt{(R_A/M_W)T_C}/\Gamma,\quad v_{eq}=C_F c^*$
Electric optimum$m_{pay}/m_{tot}=e^{-\Delta V/c}-\tfrac{\alpha_e a_0}{2}c$

Constants: $R_A=8314~\mathrm{J/(K\,kmol)}$, $g_0=9.80665~\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

20 · Problem set

  1. A kerolox stage ($I_{sp}=340$ s, $\varepsilon_s=0.09$) must deliver 5.5 km/s. Find $\Lambda$, the propellant fraction, and the payload fraction. Would two equal stages help?
  2. Derive the single-stage $\Delta V$ ceiling from the payload-fraction relation and evaluate it for hydrolox ($v_{eq}=4.4$ km/s, $\varepsilon_s=0.12$).
  3. A nozzle has $\gamma=1.22$, $\varepsilon=25$. Find $M_e$ and $p_e/p_C$. At what ambient pressure is it adapted if $p_C=60$ bar?
  4. Show that halving $M_W$ raises both $v_e$ and $v_{e,\max}$ by $\sqrt2$ while raising $\dot m$ by $\sqrt2$. Explain the design tension for thrust $=\dot m v_{eq}$.
  5. A conical nozzle has a $20^\circ$ half-angle. Compute the divergence loss factor and the percentage thrust reduction on the momentum term.
  6. An ion thruster has $I_{sp}=3000$ s and must deliver 4 km/s. Find the propellant fraction. If $\alpha_e=15$ kg/kW and the mission allows $a_0=5\times10^{-4}\,g$, is $c$ near its optimum? (Use the power-limited relation.)
  7. Estimate the gravity loss for a launcher that burns for 150 s averaging $\gamma\approx60^\circ$ flight-path angle. How much extra $\Delta V$ is that?
Selected answers

1. $\Lambda=e^{5.5/(340\cdot9.81/1000)}=e^{1.649}=5.20$; propellant fraction $1-1/\Lambda=0.808$; $\pi=(0.192-0.09)/0.91=0.112$. Two stages raise $\pi$ markedly (each ratio $\sqrt{5.20}=2.28$).
2. $\Delta V_{\max}=v_{eq}\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)=4.4\ln(1/0.12)=9.33$ km/s.
3. $M_e\approx3.85$, $p_e/p_C\approx3\times10^{-3}$; adapted when $p_a=p_e\approx0.18$ bar (≈14 km altitude).
5. $\lambda=\tfrac12(1+\cos20^\circ)=0.970$ → 3.0% reduction.
6. propellant fraction $=1-e^{-4/29.4}=0.127$.
