Historical context · the theory
The theory predates the hardware by decades

1903 · Tsiolkovsky

Derives $\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln(M_0/M_f)$ and identifies staging and liquid propellants as prerequisites for spaceflight — the same year as the first powered aeroplane.

1923 · Oberth

Die Rakete zu den Planetenräumen puts spaceflight on a rigorous physical footing and seeds European rocketry.

1926 · Goddard

Flies the first liquid-propellant rocket and pioneers the de Laval nozzle and pumps in rockets — theory meets hardware.
Historical context · the nozzle
The convergent–divergent nozzle came from steam turbines
Gustaf de Laval introduced the convergent–divergent nozzle around 1888 to expand steam to supersonic speed onto turbine blades. It is the unique quasi-1-D geometry that accelerates a compressible flow continuously through $M=1$.
  • Goddard adapted it to rockets, roughly doubling exhaust velocity over a plain orifice.
  • The whole of §1.3 is, in effect, the theory of this nozzle.
Two threads frame the lecture: Tsiolkovsky’s equation (the mass problem) and de Laval’s nozzle (the gas-dynamics problem).
Thrust & specific impulse
Two performance parameters define an engine
At the system level a propulsion device is characterised by two quantities; everything downstream either produces them or consumes them.

Thrust $F_T$  [N]

The reaction force on the vehicle. Sets acceleration and, through $F_T=\dot m\,v_{eq}$, the rate at which ΔV is delivered.

Specific impulse $I_{sp}$  [s]

Total impulse per unit propellant weight. Sets the propellant mass cost of a required ΔV.
Both derive from the steady-flow momentum balance across a control volume enclosing the engine — taken next.
Thrust & specific impulse
Thrust from a control-volume momentum balance
The integral momentum equation over a CV around the engine, with steady flow and ambient pressure acting on all surfaces except the exit plane, gives
$$F_T=\underbrace{\dot m\,v_e}_{\text{momentum flux}}+\underbrace{(p_e-p_a)\,A_e}_{\text{pressure imbalance}}$$
  • Momentum flux $\dot m v_e=\rho_e v_e^2 A_e$: the reaction to expelling mass at the exit.
  • Pressure term: the exit is generally not matched to ambient; it vanishes only when $p_e=p_a$.
Thrust & specific impulse
Altitude dependence & the equivalent jet velocity
$p_a$ falls with altitude, so the pressure term — and hence $F_T$ — rises monotonically, reaching a maximum in vacuum. It is far from negligible for large first-stage engines low down.
$$F_T=\dot m\,v_{eq},\qquad v_{eq}=v_e+\frac{(p_e-p_a)A_e}{\dot m}$$
The equivalent (effective) jet velocity $v_{eq}$ absorbs both terms, giving the compact form used throughout.

Thrust & specific impulse
Specific impulse: the rigorous definition
$$I_{sp}=\frac{1}{g_0}\,\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{t_b}F_T\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_0^{t_b}\dot m\,dt}\;\xrightarrow[\;v_{eq}=\text{const}\;]{}\;\frac{v_{eq}}{g_0}=\frac{c}{g_0}$$
Total impulse divided by propellant weight. The effective exhaust velocity $c\equiv v_{eq}=I_{sp}\,g_0$ is the velocity-space statement of the same efficiency.
The seconds arise from impulse [N·s] over weight-flow [N/s]. $g_0$ is the defined standard gravity, not local $g$ — so $I_{sp}$ is a property of the engine, independent of where it flies.

Rocket equation
Tsiolkovsky’s equation & its validity
$$\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln\frac{M_0}{M_0-M_P}=v_{eq}\ln\Lambda,\qquad \Lambda\equiv\frac{M_0}{M_f}$$
The velocity increment depends only on $v_{eq}$ and the mass ratio $\Lambda$ — not on thrust level or burn time. It is exact under three conditions:
  • no external forces (gravity, drag, side loads);
  • $v_{eq}$ constant over the burn;
  • exhaust expelled collinear with, and opposite to, the flight direction.
When these fail, ΔV still measures the specific energy imparted by the system; the losses are added explicitly below.
Rocket equation
Derivation
1
Momentum of the vehicle + expelled mass
In an inertial frame with no external forces, thrust is the only force: $M\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}=-v_{eq}\dfrac{dM}{dt}$, where $dM<0$.
2
Separate variables
$dv=-v_{eq}\dfrac{dM}{M}$. Constancy of $v_{eq}$ is precisely what allows it to leave the integral.
3
Integrate over the burn
$\displaystyle\int_0^{\Delta V}\!dv=-v_{eq}\!\int_{M_0}^{M_f}\!\frac{dM}{M}\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta V=v_{eq}\ln\dfrac{M_0}{M_f}$.
4
Variable exhaust velocity
If $v_{eq}=v_{eq}(t)$, only the integral form survives: $\Delta V=\int v_{eq}\,(-dM/M)$. This matters for throttled or dual-mode engines.
Rocket equationbeyond Delft §1.1
Mass ratio, structural & payload fractions
$$\varepsilon_s=\frac{M_s}{M_s+M_p},\qquad \pi=\frac{M_{pl}}{M_0}=\frac{\,1/\Lambda-\varepsilon_s\,}{1-\varepsilon_s},\qquad \Lambda=e^{\Delta V/v_{eq}}$$
With $M_0=M_s+M_p+M_{pl}$ and structural coefficient $\varepsilon_s$, the achievable payload fraction $\pi$ follows directly from the required $\Lambda$.
Payload vanishes when $1/\Lambda=\varepsilon_s$, i.e. at the single-stage ceiling $\Delta V_{\max}=v_{eq}\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)$. For $\varepsilon_s\approx0.1,\;v_{eq}\approx3.4$ km/s this is ~7.8 km/s — below the ~9.4 km/s LEO budget. Staging is not optional.
Rocket equation
Worked example: reaching LEO
Size a launcher for the LEO budget $\Delta V=9.4$ km/s with a kerolox stage ($I_{sp}=340$ s, structural coefficient $\varepsilon_s=0.10$).
1
Effective exhaust velocity
$v_{eq}=I_{sp}\,g_0=340\times9.807=3334$ m/s.
2
Try a single stage
$\Lambda=e^{9400/3334}=16.8$. But the ceiling is $\Delta V_{\max}=v_{eq}\ln(1/\varepsilon_s)=7.68$ km/s $<9.4$ km/s, so $\pi<0$ — impossible at any payload.
3
Two equal stages
Per stage $\Delta V=4.7$ km/s, $\Lambda=4.09$, $\pi_{stage}=(1/\Lambda-\varepsilon_s)/(1-\varepsilon_s)=16.0\%$. Compounding: $\pi=0.160^2\approx\mathbf{2.6\%}$.
4
Read the lesson
A 100-tonne two-stage vehicle delivers only ~2.6 t to LEO. The exponential — not sloppy engineering — is why launchers are almost all propellant.
Rocket equationbeyond Delft §1.1
The real ΔV budget: ideal + losses
$$\Delta V_{req}=\Delta V_{orbit}+\underbrace{\int g\sin\gamma\,dt}_{\text{gravity}}+\underbrace{\int \tfrac{D}{M}\,dt}_{\text{drag}}+\Delta V_{steer}$$
  • Gravity loss $\int g\sin\gamma\,dt$ — minimised by pitching over early (the gravity turn, $\gamma\!\to\!0$).
  • Drag loss $\int (D/M)\,dt$ — concentrated in the dense transonic lower atmosphere.
  • Together ~1.5–2 km/s, converting ~7.8 km/s orbital speed into the ~9.4 km/s budget.

Rocket equationbeyond Delft · later chapter
Staging & the optimal-staging problem
$$\Delta V_{total}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} v_{eq,i}\,\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{s,i}+(1-\varepsilon_{s,i})\,\lambda_i}$$
Each stage carries the stack above it as its payload, so mass ratios compound while ΔV adds. Maximising payload at fixed $\Delta V_{total}$ is a constrained optimisation (Lagrange multipliers); for identical stages the optimum is an equal ΔV split.
Returns diminish sharply: each stage adds structural mass, separation events and integration cost, so practical launchers stop at two or three.

Ideal rocket theory
Objective & idealised geometry
Predict the three quantities that set thrust and $I_{sp}$ — jet velocity $v_e$, mass flow $\dot m$, exit pressure $p_e$ — from chamber state $(p_C,T_C)$ and gas properties alone.
$$\varepsilon=\frac{A_e}{A^*}$$
The de Laval nozzle converts chamber enthalpy to directed kinetic energy adiabatically; its geometry is fixed by the expansion ratio $\varepsilon$.
Ideal rocket theory
The nine assumptions, in full
  1. Propellant is a perfect gas, $p=\rho\,(R_A/M_W)\,T$.
  2. Calorically ideal: $c_p,c_v,\gamma$ constant.
  3. Constant, homogeneous chemical composition.
  4. Steady flow, $\partial/\partial t=0$.
  5. Isentropic flow: adiabatic and reversible (no shocks, no friction).
  6. One-dimensional: properties vary only along the axis.
  7. Purely axial velocity at every station.
  8. No external (body) forces on the gas.
  9. Negligible chamber velocity, $v_c\approx0$.
Two additional data assumptions: the chamber state $(p_C,T_C)$ is known, and the gas properties $(\gamma,c_p,M_W)$ are known and constant through the nozzle.
Ideal rocket theory
Governing equations
$$\begin{aligned} \text{continuity:}\quad & \rho v A=\dot m=\text{const}\\[2pt] \text{momentum (Euler):}\quad & dp=-\rho v\,dv\\[2pt] \text{energy:}\quad & h+\tfrac12 v^2=h_0=c_pT_C\\[2pt] \text{state, sound, Mach:}\quad & p=\rho\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T,\ \ a^2=\gamma\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T,\ \ M=\tfrac{v}{a} \end{aligned}$$
Closed by the isentropic relations $\dfrac{T_C}{T}=1+\dfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2$ and $\dfrac{p_C}{p}=\left(\dfrac{T_C}{T}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$. Every result below is algebra on this set.
Nozzle flow
Area–velocity relation — derivation
$$\frac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\,\frac{dv}{v}$$
1
Log-differentiate continuity
$\rho vA=$const $\Rightarrow \dfrac{d\rho}{\rho}+\dfrac{dv}{v}+\dfrac{dA}{A}=0$.
2
Euler + speed of sound
$dp=-\rho v\,dv$ and $a^2=dp/d\rho \Rightarrow \dfrac{d\rho}{\rho}=-M^2\dfrac{dv}{v}$.
3
Combine
Eliminating $d\rho/\rho$ gives $\dfrac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\dfrac{dv}{v}$.
4
Read the sign
$M\lt1$: accelerate by converging. $M\gt1$: accelerate by diverging. $M=1$ only where $dA=0$ — the throat.
Nozzle flow
Jet velocity — derivation
1
Energy with $v_c\approx0$
$\tfrac12 v_e^2=h_C-h_e=c_p(T_C-T_e)\;\Rightarrow\;v_e=\sqrt{2c_p(T_C-T_e)}$.
2
Isentropic temperature ratio
$\dfrac{T_e}{T_C}=\left(\dfrac{p_e}{p_C}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}$.
3
Substitute $c_p=\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\dfrac{R_A}{M_W}$
gives the closed form below.
$$v_e=\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\,\frac{R_A}{M_W}\,T_C\left[\,1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_C}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}$$
Levers: $v_e\propto\sqrt{T_C}$, $v_e\propto1/\sqrt{M_W}$ (dominant — hydrogen), and rises with deeper expansion (smaller $p_e/p_C$, diminishing returns). Limit $p_e/p_C\!\to\!0$ gives $v_{e,\max}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T_C}$.

Nozzle flow
Choked mass flow — derivation
1
Evaluate at the sonic throat
$\dot m=\rho^\ast a^\ast A^\ast$ with $M=1$, so $v^\ast=a^\ast=\sqrt{\gamma\tfrac{R_A}{M_W}T^\ast}$.
2
Isentropic throat ratios
$\dfrac{T^\ast}{T_C}=\dfrac{2}{\gamma+1}$, $\dfrac{\rho^\ast}{\rho_C}=\left(\dfrac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}$, $\rho_C=\dfrac{p_C}{(R_A/M_W)T_C}$.
3
Collect
All the $\gamma$-factors group into the Vandenkerckhove function $\Gamma(\gamma)$.
$$\dot m=\frac{p_C\,A^*}{\sqrt{\frac{R_A}{M_W}T_C}}\,\Gamma(\gamma),\qquad \Gamma=\sqrt{\gamma\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}}$$
Choked: once the throat is sonic, $\dot m$ is fixed by chamber state and $A^*$ alone — independent of back-pressure. Note the sign clash with $v_e$: high $\dot m$ wants high $M_W$ and low $T_C$.
Expansion
Area ratio fixes the exit state
$$\frac{A}{A^*}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}$$
On the supersonic branch this is monotonic, so a given $\varepsilon=A_e/A^*$ selects a unique exit Mach number $M_e$ — and through the isentropic relations, a unique $p_e/p_C$ and $T_e/T_C$.
This is the rigorous content of “geometry fixes the exit pressure”: $\;\varepsilon\Rightarrow M_e\Rightarrow p_e/p_C$. The dependence on $\gamma$ is weak; larger $\varepsilon$ gives lower $p_e$.
Expansion
Over-, adapted & under-expanded operation
  • over-expanded   $p_e\lt p_a$ — oblique shocks at the lip; if $p_e/p_a$ is small enough the boundary layer separates inside the nozzle.
  • adapted   $p_e=p_a$ — pressure term zero; thrust is maximal for the given geometry.
  • under-expanded   $p_e\gt p_a$ — Prandtl–Meyer expansion continues outside the nozzle.
Since $p_e$ is fixed by $\varepsilon$ but $p_a$ falls with altitude, a fixed nozzle is adapted at exactly one altitude — the first-stage/upper-stage compromise.

Expansionbeyond Delft §1.3 · pt 2
Performance coefficients: $C_F$ and $c^*$
$$c^*=\frac{p_C A^*}{\dot m}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\sqrt{\frac{R_A}{M_W}T_C},\qquad C_F=\frac{F_T}{p_C A^*}$$
The two coefficients factor engine performance: $c^*$ isolates the chamber + propellant (energy release, gas properties), $C_F$ isolates the nozzle (expansion quality). Their product is the effective exhaust velocity:
$$v_{eq}=C_F\,c^*\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad I_{sp}=\frac{C_F\,c^*}{g_0}$$
Ideal $C_F=\Gamma\sqrt{\dfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\!\left[1-\!\left(\tfrac{p_e}{p_C}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}+\varepsilon\,\dfrac{p_e-p_a}{p_C}$ — the very quantity the expansion explorer reports.
Expansionbeyond Delft §1.3 · ch 6
Deviations from ideal rocket theory
Real engines fall short of ideal predictions; the shortfalls are collected into efficiencies on $c^*$ (combustion) and $C_F$ (nozzle), each typically 0.90–0.99.
  • Finite-rate chemistry — real expansion lies between the frozen and shifting-equilibrium bounds, changing $T_e$, composition and $v_e$.
  • Real-gas / variable properties — $\gamma,c_p,M_W$ drift with temperature and composition along the nozzle.
  • Friction & heat transfer — boundary layers and wall cooling make the flow non-isentropic and non-adiabatic.
  • Divergence loss — non-axial exit flow; a conical half-angle $\alpha$ gives $\lambda=\tfrac12(1+\cos\alpha)$ (e.g. $\lambda\approx0.983$ at $15^\circ$).
  • Two-phase flow — condensed particles (Al₂O₃ in solids) lag the gas thermally and in velocity.
Design practice: apply $\eta_{c^*}$ and $\eta_{C_F}$ to the ideal results — the theory sets the ceiling, the efficiencies set the delivery.
