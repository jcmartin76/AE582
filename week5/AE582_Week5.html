Recall from Week 4 · the method
Mission analysis, in one thread
Mission analysis turns objectives into a feasible, costed concept and its design drivers. Every regime this week runs the same thread — objectives → drivers → concept → budgets → trade → baseline — with a different budget doing the driving. Orbital dynamics supplies the numbers; mission analysis assembles the decision.
Recall from Week 4 · the tools
Design drivers, budgets & the trade

Design drivers

The few requirements that, once set, dictate most of the vehicle — required orbit, coverage/revisit, lifetime, pointing, power/eclipse, data rate. Find them early; everything downstream serves them.

Budgets & margins

Size each candidate against the budgets — coverage, ΔV/propellant, power/eclipse, link, lifetime, timeline, mass — each carried with margin. A concept "closes" only when all balance at once.
Trade study: score architectures on figures of merit × weights → the weighted winner is the baseline, and the reasons it won are its drivers.
This week we spend exactly these tools on five regimes. In each, watch for the one budget that binds — that is the regime's signature driver.

GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — objectives
Break the monopoly: private international broadcasting

The mission objective

Provide commercial international TV & data broadcasting across the Americas and the Atlantic — the first privately-owned international communications satellite, breaking the Intelsat monopoly (René Anselmo, PanAmSat, launched 1988).

Why it's a mission-analysis problem

A one-satellite business with no customers at start: the analysis has to turn "sell broadcasting over the Atlantic" into a specific spacecraft, orbit slot, payload and budget that closes commercially.
We'll walk PAS-1 through the full flow — objective → drivers → concept → budgets → trade → baseline — the same six steps we'll use for every mission today.
GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — drivers
What the objective forces on the design
DriverBecause…
A GEO slot over the service regionfixed over 45°W, one satellite sees the Americas + the Atlantic to Europe
Transponder capacity & bandrevenue = channels sold → number of transponders; C-band (wide, rain-robust) + Ku (higher power, smaller dishes)

The governing figure of merit here is commercial viability — cost and revenue, not raw performance. Watch it shape every later step.
GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — concept
One GEO comsat at 45° West
  • A single three-axis-stabilised GEO communications satellite parked at 45°W (RCA AS-3000 bus, ~1220 kg).
  • Payload: 18 transponders (12 C-band + 6 Ku-band) with fixed beams shaped to the Americas / Atlantic.
  • Delivery: launched into GTO, then an apogee boost motor circularises and removes inclination at apogee.
The concept is deliberately conventional — a proven bus and a standard GTO→GEO insertion — because the risk budget is spent on the business, not on new technology.
GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — budgets
Sizing the satellite: link, power, station-keeping

Link & power

The link budget (EIRP, coverage beams) sets antenna size and transponder power; the power budget sizes the solar arrays plus batteries to ride through the daily eclipse.

Station-keeping ΔV

Holding the 45°W slot for 10 yr is dominated by north–south control (luni-solar, ~50 m/s/yr) with cheap east–west (triaxiality) trims — this fuel load sizes the tank. Quantify it →

GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — trade
The trades that actually decided PAS-1
  • Coverage vs capacity vs band: how many C- vs Ku-band transponders, and which beams — set by the target markets and the revenue plan.
  • Buy vs build: Anselmo bought a cut-rate cancelled satellite (~$45M vs ~$80M) instead of a bespoke build — cost over customisation.
  • Launcher: with the US grounded after Challenger, he took the cheapest ride — the maiden Ariane 44LP — accepting schedule/reliability risk.
  • Risk: insured the satellite at only half its value, and signed an anchor customer (CNN, Latin America) to close the business.
Notice the trades are cost-, schedule- and risk-driven — exactly what you'd expect when the design driver is commercial viability, not performance.

GEO · PanAmSat PAS-1 — baseline
The baseline that flew

The baseline

An 1220-kg AS-3000 comsat, 18 transponders (12 C + 6 Ku), at 45°W GEO, ~10-yr design life, on an Ariane 44LP. It broke the Intelsat monopoly and — with CNN and Gulf-War coverage — was near full capacity by 1992 (achieved 13 yr).

The flow, for GEO

objective (private broadcasting) → driver (slot + capacity + viability) → concept (one comsat @45°W) → budgets (link, power, N–S ΔV) → trade (buy-vs-build, launcher, risk) → baseline (PAS-1).
Constellations · SunRISE — objectives
See the Sun's radio bursts to forecast space weather

The mission objective

Detect and map solar radio bursts (0.1–25 MHz) to pinpoint where solar energetic particles are accelerated — improving warning of space-weather events that endanger astronauts, satellites and power grids. (NASA heliophysics; PI Univ. Michigan.)

The catch

Those long wavelengths are blocked by Earth's ionosphere — you cannot observe them from the ground at all.
A different kind of constellation: not for ground coverage, but to build a telescope in space. Watch how that objective forces a multi-satellite design.
Constellations · SunRISE — drivers
Why the objective demands many satellites
1
Get above the ionosphere
The wavelengths of interest are blocked below ~10 MHz from the ground → the instrument must be in space.
2
Resolution needs a huge aperture
Angular resolution ∝ wavelength / aperture. At these long wavelengths you need an aperture kilometres across — impossible as one dish.
3
Synthesise the aperture → a constellation
Interferometry combines several small antennas spread over that distance; the baseline between the farthest elements is the aperture. Adequate imaging (u–v coverage) needs several elements → a constellation is the only way.
The signature driver here is the interferometric baseline (and the number of elements) — the constellation analogue of "degree of coverage".
Constellations · SunRISE — concept
Six SmallSats as one virtual telescope
  • Six identical SmallSats (cereal-box size), each deploying four 2.5-m antenna booms.
  • They fly in a loose ~10 km formation slightly above GEO (~35 000 km).
  • Signals are downlinked (DSN) and combined on the ground by interferometry into one virtual 10-km radio telescope.
Above GEO is a stable "graveyard" region — negligible drag and gentle perturbations — so the formation is cheap to maintain.
Constellations · SunRISE — budgets
What the six-satellite design must balance

Imaging budget

The number of elements and their separations set the aperture (resolution) and the u–v coverage (image quality). Six gives enough baselines to locate bursts without a large, costly array.

Formation, timing & data

Formation-keeping ΔV is small (stable super-GEO orbit); the hard parts are precise inter-satellite timing and getting six data streams down via the DSN to correlate.
Here the binding budget is imaging fidelity per satellite, not coverage or ΔV — the driver reshapes which budget matters.
Constellations · SunRISE — trade
Elements vs fidelity vs cost
  • Number of satellites: more elements → better u–v coverage and images, but higher cost. Six SmallSats is the knee — cheap, redundant, and enough baselines.
  • Formation size: a wider baseline sharpens resolution but complicates formation control and timing.
  • Orbit: super-GEO (stable, no drag, cheap station-keeping) over LEO (drag, short usable baselines).
  • Contrast — a coverage constellation (GNSS) trades coverage vs cost via footprint & number of planes instead. Explore that version →
Same trade shape as any constellation — buy capability with satellites — but the "capability" is imaging fidelity, not ground coverage.

Constellations · SunRISE — baseline
The baseline constellation

The baseline

Six SmallSats in a ~10 km formation slightly above GEO, launching as a rideshare (~2026) — the smallest, cheapest constellation that synthesises an adequate radio aperture in space.

The flow, for constellations

objective (map solar radio bursts) → driver (baseline & # elements, above the ionosphere) → concept (6 SmallSats, super-GEO formation) → budget (u–v coverage, timing, data) → trade (elements vs fidelity vs cost) → baseline (6-sat SunRISE).
Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — objectives
Is Europa's ocean habitable?

The mission objective

Assess the habitability of Europa's subsurface ocean — determine the ice-shell thickness and ocean interaction, the composition, and the geology — via 49 close flybys. NASA's first mission to an ocean world (launched Oct 2024, Falcon Heavy).

The setting

Europa orbits Jupiter, deep in the giant's lethal radiation belts, ~5 AU from the Sun and ~2.9 billion km from Earth. Everything about the design flows from that environment.
The largest planetary spacecraft NASA has built — because the objective sits in one of the harshest places in the solar system.
Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — drivers
Radiation, distance, and launch energy
DriverConsequence for the design
Jupiter's intense radiationorbit Jupiter, not Europa — dip in for brief flybys then retreat; shield electronics in a radiation vault
~5 AU distance / low solar fluxenormous solar arrays to make power far from the Sun (the power driver)
Huge launch energy (C₃) to Jupitera gravity-assist trajectory + a fully expendable heavy launcher (Falcon Heavy)
2.9 billion km / faint signallarge high-gain antenna & the Deep Space Network for the data budget
The governing driver is radiation dose — it dictates the whole flyby architecture. Distance and launch energy dictate power and trajectory.
Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — concept
A Jupiter orbiter that flies by Europa 49 times
  • Orbit Jupiter on a long, looping tour; make 49 close Europa flybys — collect data on each dip, then swing back out of the worst radiation.
  • Reach Jupiter via a Mars–Earth gravity-assist (MEGA) cruise: Mars (Mar 2025) → Earth (Dec 2026) → Jupiter (Apr 2030), ~5½ yr.
  • A Ganymede gravity assist eases Jupiter capture (a ~6-hour insertion burn).
Orbiting Jupiter rather than Europa is the concept-level answer to the radiation driver — it trades some science continuity for a spacecraft that survives to do the science at all.
Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — budgets
ΔV, power, radiation and time

ΔV & timeline

Budget: launch C₃ (expendable Falcon Heavy) + deep-space maneuvers + the Jupiter-orbit-insertion burn (~6 h). The gravity assists buy the arrival energy the launcher can't. Timeline: fixed launch window (Oct 2024) → 5½-yr cruise.

Power & radiation

The power budget sizes the giant solar arrays for 5 AU; the radiation-dose budget sets the vault shielding and the flyby-not-orbit tour so total dose stays survivable.
Four budgets bind at once here — ΔV, power, radiation and timeline — and the concept exists to keep all four inside their limits.
Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — trade
The choices that shaped Clipper
  • Direct vs gravity assist: a direct shot needs impossible launch energy → the MEGA route trades ~2 extra years for a feasible C₃.
  • Orbit Europa vs orbit Jupiter: orbiting Europa gives continuous science but a fatal radiation dose → orbit Jupiter and fly by 49× instead.
  • Solar vs nuclear (RTG) power: chose solar (cost/availability) at the price of very large arrays.
Every trade is dominated by the radiation and launch-energy drivers. Explore the gravity-assist geometry that makes the trajectory possible →

Interplanetary · Europa Clipper — baseline
The baseline mission

The baseline

Falcon Heavy (expendable) + a Mars–Earth gravity-assist cruise → Jupiter arrival Apr 2030 → a Jupiter orbiter making 49 Europa flybys (2030–2034), solar-powered, electronics in a radiation vault.

The flow, for interplanetary

objective (Europa's habitability) → drivers (radiation, distance, C₃) → concept (Jupiter orbiter, 49 flybys, MEGA) → budgets (ΔV, power, dose, time) → trade (assist vs direct; flyby vs orbit; solar vs RTG) → baseline (Europa Clipper).
Other regimes — same flow, different driver
The method carries to every regime

Small-body missions

Objective: orbit/land on an asteroid or comet. Driver: tiny, irregular gravity & navigation risk. Concept: slow proximity ops with a modelled (Mascon) gravity field. e.g. NEAR, Rosetta, OSIRIS-REx.

Low-thrust missions

Objective: reach the orbit on minimal propellant. Driver: trip time vs propellant. Concept: a continuous-thrust spiral with a steering law. e.g. Dawn, all-electric GEO.
Same six steps — objectives → drivers → concept → budgets → trade → baseline — only the driving budget changes. That is the whole discipline.
Week 5 · summary
The regimes on one page

What drove each regime

  • GEO: N–S station-keeping (~50 m/s/yr) dominates the fuel budget.
  • Constellations: smallest Walker set that meets the coverage requirement.
  • Interplanetary: patch the conics; capture-orbit shape trades against ΔV; gravity assists buy ΔV with time.

…and the rest

  • Small-body: irregular gravity & nav risk, not ΔV, drive the design.
  • Low-thrust: trade trip time for propellant; spiral, don't impulse.
  • Operational budgets: visibility, eclipse, windows — and the rocket equation pays the bill.
One line: mission analysis is one method with many faces — in every regime you find the driving budget, run the trade, and let orbital dynamics supply the numbers.
