The whole idea in one sentence

Keep every component inside its allowable temperature range, in every phase of the mission, with margin, using the least mass, power and cost.

Where thermal sits in the bus

Thermal is one of the bus subsystems in Fortescue's Fig. 1.3 (chapters 1 and 6). It depends on almost every other subsystem: power gives it dissipations, structures gives it the layout, AOCS gives it the attitude, and it hands heater power and sensor channels back to them.

Week 9 · Session 9.1

Why spacecraft need thermal control

Two main reasons

  • Performance and life. Electronics, batteries and mechanisms work within narrow ranges (roughly −15 °C to +50 °C). Outside them they drift, fail or age faster.
  • Structural and optical integrity. Materials expand and contract; gradients distort structures. Telescopes may need stability to a fraction of a kelvin.

Why space makes it hard

  • No air: no convection. Radiation is the only way to reject heat.
  • Full Sun near Earth heats surfaces towards ~390 K; shadow drops towards ~3 K.
  • The Sun and Earth move around the spacecraft every orbit, and eclipses switch the Sun off.
  • Cold detectors and warm electronics on one vehicle mean several thermal zones.
  • No repairs after launch, and heat pipes behave differently in 1 g during test.

Week 9 · Session 9.1

Thermal control is a mission driver, not an afterthought

First, how is it done on Earth?

A desktop CPU (Intel Core i9-9980XE) dissipates up to 165 W and must keep its die below 84 °C. The solution: a heat pipe carries the heat to a finned radiator, and a fan blows air through it.

In orbit there is no air for the fan. The same heat pipe works (better, with no gravity), but the "radiator" must now reject everything by radiation, so it is much larger and must not see the Sun.

The workflow is the same for every mission: mission objectives → orbit, attitude and requirements (power, temperatures, gradients, stability) → thermal control methods and models (worst cases) → testing (balance, cycling, solar simulation).

Week 9 · Session 9.1

Temperature definitions: predicted, design, acceptance, qualification

  • Calculated range: what the thermal model predicts for the nominal worst cases.
  • Predicted range = calculated ± uncertainty (model, properties, dissipations). Typically ±10 K early, reduced after the model is correlated to test.
  • Design range (allowable flight temperatures): the predicted range must fit inside it.
  • Acceptance range = design ± acceptance margin (≈ 5 K). Every flight unit is tested here.
  • Qualification range = acceptance ± qualification margin (≈ 5 K). Tested once, on a qualification unit.
NASA GSFC practice (GOLD rules): hold 5 °C margin to limits, or, for heater-controlled parts, use no more than 70 % of heater capacity in the worst cold case.

Week 9 · Session 9.1

Thermo-optical properties: two wavebands, two numbers

  • Incident radiation is absorbed, reflected or transmitted: α + ρ + τ = 1.
  • Kirchhoff's law: at a given wavelength and direction, absorptance equals emittance, α(λ) = ε(λ).
  • The Sun (≈ 5800 K) emits at 0.25–2.5 µm. A spacecraft (≈ 300 K) emits at ≈ 3–40 µm. The bands barely overlap.
  • So engineers use two grey numbers: α, the solar absorptance, and ε, the IR emittance (which is also the IR absorptance).
αvis + ρvis + τvis = 1   ·   εIR + ρIR + τIR = 1

ECSS convention: α weighted by the solar spectrum over 0.25–2.5 µm; ε weighted by a 300 K blackbody over 3–20 µm.

Week 9 · Session 9.1

Two refinements: solar cells, and surfaces that are not grey

Solar cells from a thermal point of view

  • The array keeps (α − η)Qi; the electrical part ηQi becomes heat later, in the boxes that use it. Solar power only moves heat in space and time.
  • So a converting array runs cooler (effective αeff = α − η). If the bus stops drawing power, the array heats up: that is the hot case ("not converting").

Real surfaces are not perfectly grey or diffuse

  • Direction. Emittance of non-metals is high and fairly flat with angle; metals are low and rise at grazing angles before dropping to zero.
  • Wavelength and temperature. A coating measured at 300 K may behave differently at 1000 K, when its own emission moves into the "solar" band. At high temperature ε tends towards α (relevant for Parker's shield).
  • Specular vs diffuse. Models split reflectance into ρ = ρs + ρd. Mirrors, OSRs and polished metals are specular; paints are diffuse.
  • BSDF (bidirectional scattering distribution function) describes full angular behaviour. Earth-observation instruments use it for calibration diffusers (Sentinel-4 UVN, MTG IRS).
Week 9 · Session 9.1

Derivation: the spacecraft energy balance

Treat the spacecraft as one isothermal node of mass m, specific heat cp and total radiating area Arad. Apply the first law: no work is done, so storage = in − out.

  1. Heat in from the environment and from the electronics:
    Qin = α Gs Asun + α Gs a F AE + ε qI F AE + Qint
  2. Heat out, radiated to deep space (Tspace4 ≈ 0): Qout = ε σ Arad T4
  3. Energy balance for the node:
    m cp dT/dt = Qin(t) − ε σ Arad T4
  4. Steady state (dT/dt = 0) gives the equilibrium temperature. Positive right-hand side: T rises; negative: it falls; zero: it stays.
    Teq = [ Qin / (ε σ Arad) ]1/4
  5. Here F = (RE / (RE + h))² is the view factor from a nadir-facing plate to Earth, and a F is the simplest albedo model (valid near the subsolar point; it falls towards the terminator).
Result: Teq ∝ (Qin/εA)1/4. Doubling the heat load raises the absolute temperature by only 19 %.

Worked example — equilibrium temperature of a white 1U CubeSat

Given

  • 1U cube, 10 cm sides; face area A = 0.01 m², radiating area 6A = 0.06 m²
  • Circular LEO, h = 500 km; one face to Sun, one face to nadir
  • White paint α = 0.20, ε = 0.85
  • Hot case: Gs = 1414, a = 0.4, qI = 261 W/m²
  • Qint = 0.73 W (the CubeSat power table)

Find

The hot-case equilibrium temperature. Is white paint a good choice?

  1. F = ( RE / (RE + h) )²View factor: F = (6378/6878)² = 0.860
  2. Qsun = α Gs ASun: 0.20 × 1414 × 0.01 = 2.83 W
  3. Qalb = α Gs a F AAlbedo: 0.20 × 1414 × 0.4 × 0.860 × 0.01 = 0.97 W
  4. QIR = ε qI F A;  Qin = Qsun + Qalb + QIR + QintEarth IR: 0.85 × 261 × 0.860 × 0.01 = 1.91 W; total Qin = 6.44 W
  5. T = [ Qin / (ε σ Arad) ]1/4T = [6.44 / (0.85 × 5.67·10−8 × 0.06)]1/4 = 217 K
Answer: T ≈ 217 K (−56 °C) in the hot case — far too cold for the battery.
Real CubeSats are covered in solar cells (α ≈ 0.9, ε ≈ 0.85): the same sum gives 19.7 W and 287 K (14 °C). In eclipse with no Sun or albedo the equilibrium would drop to ≈ 164 K, but thermal mass keeps the real swing much smaller (two slides ahead).

Week 9 · Session 9.1

View factors: how much of surface i sees surface j

Geometry only: the view factor Fij

Fij = (1/Ai) ∫∫ cos θi cos θj / (πr²) dAj dAi
  • Fraction of diffuse energy leaving i that hits j directly. Ranges from 0 to 1.
  • Reciprocity: AiFij = AjFji. Enclosure: Σj Fij = 1.
  • Inside a cube, any face sees any other face with F = 0.2.

Geometry + surfaces: the exchange factor Bij

  • Fraction of energy emitted by i that is absorbed by j, directly or after any number of reflections.
  • Qij = σ εi Ai Bij (Ti4 − Tj4); the product εiAiBij is the radiative coupling (GR, "Radk").
  • For black surfaces B = F. Question: can Bij ≠ 0 when Fij = 0? (Yes, by reflection.)

How B is computed

  • Gebhart (1957), for diffuse grey isothermal surfaces: Bij = Fijεj + Σk Fik(1 − εk)Bkj, or B = (I − F + β)−1β with βij = Fijεj.
  • Monte Carlo ray tracing for real geometry: fire many rays from i and count the fraction absorbed by j, Bij ≈ Nij/Ni. Also used for contamination and computer graphics.
  • A full satellite model has 5 000–20 000 surfaces and hundreds of thousands to millions of couplings, recomputed at every orbit point.
Why the book-keeping explodes: in the GSFC cube with an ε = 0.9 floor, ε = 0.1 walls and an open top, a single emitted beam splits at every wall into absorbed and reflected parts, and every reflected part splits again.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Coatings in practice: choosing, protecting, and ageing

What coatings are used for

  • External radiator: white paint or OSR if it sees the Sun; black paint if it never does.
  • Keep a part warm: low ε (and high α if sunlit), e.g. gold or VDA tape.
  • Internal: black (high ε) to even out temperatures between boxes; low ε to isolate a sensitive unit; black also kills stray light.
  • First-surface mirror (metal on top): low α, low ε. Second-surface mirror / OSR (metal behind glass or Teflon): low α, high ε.

Overcoats

  • Gold instead of aluminium against corrosion · SiOx against atomic oxygen · ITO against static charge.

The space environment degrades them

  • UV, charged particles, atomic oxygen, contamination, micrometeoroids, launch-site corrosion.
  • Mostly α increases; ε is nearly unchanged. White paint suffers from UV and particles; metallized Teflon from particles and contamination.
  • So each coating has beginning-of-life (BOL) and end-of-life (EOL) values. Size radiators with EOL α (hot case) and check heaters with BOL α (cold case).
  • Evidence: Kapton samples flown for six years on NASA's Long Duration Exposure Facility came back visibly eroded.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Radiators, sink temperature and louvers

Radiators

  • A surface dedicated to rejecting heat: high ε, low α if sunlit, high conductivity to spread heat across it.
  • Put it where the Sun never shines: often the anti-Sun or wake side, or a side kept cold by a yaw flip at β = 0.
  • Stake the claim early: moving a radiator after layout freezes is painful. An oversized radiator can be blanketed late; an undersized one cannot grow.
  • Efficiency η = (Tavg/Tmax)4, aim for 80–90 %. Thicker skins, doublers or embedded heat pipes raise it.

Sink temperature

The temperature a massless, back-insulated plate reaches in the real environment. Radiators then size against σε(Trad4 − Tsink4). Below ≈ 200 K the sink barely matters for a 300 K radiator, which is why LN₂ shrouds at 80 K are fine for testing avionics.

Louvers — like opening a window

  • Low-ε blades over a high-ε radiator. Bimetallic springs open them when warm.
  • Effective emittance changes about 6× between closed and open (rule of thumb).
  • Save heater power when a unit is off or the spacecraft is far from the Sun. Flown on Rosetta, MESSENGER and New Horizons; now used less.

Example: the ISS

Its white radiator panels are mounted perpendicular to the solar arrays, so they stay edge-on to the Sun while ammonia loops carry heat out to them.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Heaters: the most-used thermal component

Why and how

  • Replace heat when a unit is off, warm a unit before switch-on, control gradients and stability.
  • Kapton film (thermofoil) heaters bonded and over-taped with aluminium tape; cartridge heaters bolted in.
  • Control by thermostat (bimetallic switch, 5–7 °C deadband, > 100 000 cycles) or by software (relay, PID, fine control, commandable).
  • Two thermostats in series so one failed-closed unit cannot lock a heater on; quad-redundant for both failure modes.
Sizing rule (GSFC): worst cold case at minimum bus voltage must use ≤ 70 % of heater capacity. At maximum voltage check current and watt density.

Radioisotope heater units (RHUs)

  • ≈ 1 W each from Pu-238 decay, no power or wiring needed. Used on Galileo, Huygens and the Mars Exploration Rovers.
  • How many? Start from the cold-case heat deficit of each zone: RHUs = deficit ÷ 1 W, plus margin.
  • Radiation? Pu-238 is an α emitter, so the graphite and platinum-rhodium clad stops almost all of it. Low-level γ and neutrons remain and are included in the radiation budget; electronics are not specially hardened for them.

CubeSat reality check

In the CubeSat power table the battery heater (5 W) is the largest single load after the antenna deployer. Heaters are often the first thing cut from a CubeSat power budget.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Heat pipes: evaporation plus capillarity

  • A sealed tube with a wick (often grooved aluminium extrusion) and a working fluid. Heat boils fluid at the evaporator, vapour flows to the condenser, condenses and releases heat, and the wick pumps liquid back.
  • CCHP (constant conductance): passive, no moving parts.
  • VCHP: a heated reservoir of non-condensable gas blocks part of the condenser to regulate temperature.
  • LHP (loop heat pipe): wick only in the evaporator, flexible lines, much higher capacity, ±1 °C control; needs start-up heaters.
  • Ammonia is the usual fluid near room temperature: boiling range matches electronics, high latent heat, low freezing point. Water freezes at 0 °C.
100 Wthrough a 10 mm × 1 m heat pipe (≈ 50 g) with < 0.5 K gradient

Testing and CubeSats

  • Capillary forces are weaker than gravity: in ground test the pipe must be level, or the evaporator lower (reflux mode). 3D-bent pipes are hard to test.
  • Standard on commercial satellites. On CubeSats they are uncommon but feasible: small flat and flexible heat pipes exist, and the real limit is the heat load, which a 1U rarely has.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Active cooling and pumped loops

Thermoelectric coolers (Peltier)

  • Reverse of the thermocouple (Seebeck) effect. Compact, no vibration, reliable, only 6–10 % efficient.
  • Hubble STIS: lifts 0.3 W at −80 °C and rejects 17.7 W at +20 °C.
  • Stages stack as a pyramid, since each lower stage removes the heat and the power of the stage above. Practical floor ≈ −130 °C.

Cryocoolers

  • Stirling or pulse-tube cycles for detectors below ≈ −120 °C. Cause vibration and jitter; need control electronics.
  • JWST MIRI needs ≈ 7 K, colder than the other instruments, so it has its own cryocooler. CubeSat versions exist at about half a U.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Latent heat and ablation: PCMs and thermal protection systems

Phase-change materials

  • Paraffin wax in a sealed box melts while a unit is on and refreezes when it is off, holding it near the melt temperature.
  • Also damps the sunlight–eclipse cycle in LEO, or day–night on an airless body.
  • Heavy and poorly conducting (needs internal fins), so used only where stability justifies it.
  • Herschel's superfluid helium cryostat is the extreme case: slow evaporation (≈ 2.5 mg/s) cooled the detectors, and the helium supply set the mission lifetime.

Thermal protection system (entry)

  • Blunt bodies push the bow shock forward, away from the surface: heat load falls as drag coefficient rises.
  • Ablators char and erode, carrying heat away and blocking shock-layer radiation (Apollo, Huygens, Stardust PICA).
  • Reusable tiles (Space Shuttle, > 20 000 inspected each flight).
  • Peak fluxes range from ≈ 10 W/cm² (Viking, Mars Pathfinder) to ≈ 30 000 W/cm² (Galileo at Jupiter). Half of the ablators NASA used in 40 years are no longer made.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

The thermal design process

Five inputs thermal needs from others

  1. Temperature limits, operating and non-operating, from the hardware owner (never from thermal predictions).
  2. Dissipations, current best estimate and maximum expected value; add ≈ 30 % if only CBE exists.
  3. Physical layout (CAD) from the mechanical engineer.
  4. Orbit and attitude: β range, pointing, safe mode, launch, burns.
  5. Stability and gradient requirements, stated precisely: "0.1 K over 10 min", not "0.1 K".

Then answer the first questions

  • How many thermal zones? Which boxes share limits and can share a radiator?
  • Is there a permanently cold side? Where do radiators go and how does heat reach them (panel, strap, heat pipe)?
  • Which coatings, where? Where is isolation needed?
  • How big are the radiators (hot case) and heaters (cold case)?
  • Does anything need active cooling?
  • Where are the sensors (thermistors, PRTs, diodes)?
  • Can it be tested on the ground?

Then: analysis → procurement → integration → test → model correlation → flight. H. Peabody, NASA GSFC (2022).

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Derivation — sizing a radiator and its heater by hand

  1. Radiator temperature. Start from the upper limit, subtract margin (5 °C) and the transport drop ΔTtr (strap, heat pipe), then include fin efficiency η:
    Trad = η1/4 (Tmax − 5 − ΔTtr)   [in kelvin]
  2. Hot-case balance. Dissipation plus absorbed environment equals radiated heat:
    1.3 Qdiss + qabsA = ε σ A (Trad4 − Tsink4)
  3. Solve for area. With a clean view to deep space (qabs = 0, Tsink ≈ 0):
    Arad = 1.3 Qdiss / (ε σ Trad4)
  4. Cold case. The same radiator now runs at the lower limit plus margin, Tlow. The heater supplies what dissipation and environment do not:
    Qhtr = ε σ Arad Tlow4 − Qdiss,min − qabsA  →  installed = Qhtr / 0.7
The tension: a radiator big enough for the hot case is too big for the cold case, and the difference is paid in heater power.
Week 9 · Session 9.2 · Worked example

Worked example — radiator and survival heater for a 100 W box

Given

  • Box dissipates 100 W (CBE); allow +30 % growth
  • Upper limit +40 °C, 5 °C margin, 3 °C transport drop
  • Radiator efficiency 85 %, ε = 0.87, clear view to deep space
  • Survival (box off): lower non-op limit −20 °C

Find

Radiator area and the installed survival heater power.

  1. Tmax,eff = 40 − 5 − 3 = 32 °C = 305.15 K
  2. Trad = 0.851/4 × 305.15 = 293.0 K (19.85 °C)
  3. A = 130 / (0.87 × 5.67·10−8 × 293.04) = 130 / 363.5 = 0.358 m²
  4. Cold: Tlow = −20 + 3 = −17 °C = 256.15 K; Qhtr = 0.87σ(0.358)(256.15)4 = 75.9 W
  5. Installed at 70 % duty: 75.9 / 0.7 = 108.4 W at minimum voltage
Answer: A ≈ 0.36 m², survival heater ≈ 108 W.
Power budget: the 130 W allocation for the box is already booked on the array, so a 108 W heater used only while the box is off adds no array demand, but it still needs a power service, relays and harness.

In-class exercise — an electronics box on the cold side

Problem

An electronics box dissipates 15 W (minimum) to 40 W (maximum) and must stay between −10 °C and +40 °C. The orbit has β from 0° to 90° and the spacecraft points nadir. The box is mounted on a "cold side" that never sees the Sun but always has a glancing view of Earth, with exchange factor B ≈ 0.2 to Earth. Radiator ε = 0.85; hold 5 °C margin.

Work with your neighbour

  • Hot case: 40 + 0.2 × 0.85 × 243 × A = σ × 0.85 × A × 3084. Find A.
  • Cold case: Earth IR 208 W/m², box at 15 W, radiator at 268 K. Find Qhtr.
  • Size the installed heater with the 70 % rule.

Try it first, then reveal the worked solution.

  1. Hot: σ·0.85·3084 = 433.7 W/m²; absorbed Earth IR 0.2·0.85·243 = 41.3 W/m²; A = 40 / (433.7 − 41.3) = 0.102 m²
  2. Cold: out σ·0.85·0.102·2684 = 25.3 W; in 15 + 0.2·0.85·208·0.102 = 18.6 W
  3. Qhtr = 25.3 − 18.6 ≈ 6.7 W; installed 6.7 / 0.7 ≈ 9.6 W
Answer: radiator ≈ 0.1 m², heater ≈ 10 W. Verify next with a model: a cube with α = ε = 1 in the real orbit gives the true environmental loads.
Week 9 · Session 9.2

Thermal modelling: the lumped-parameter method

  • Split the spacecraft into isothermal nodes, like an electrical network. Temperature fields are smoother than stress fields, so hundreds to a few thousand nodes are enough.
  • Heat balance for each node i:
Ci dTi/dt = Qi + Σj GLij(Tj − Ti) + Σj σGRij(Tj4 − Ti4)
  • Ci = ρcV [J/K] · GL = conductive coupling [W/K] · GR = εAB radiative coupling [m²]
  • GMM (geometric model): computes GR and orbital solar, albedo and IR fluxes by ray tracing.
  • TMM (thermal model): capacitances, dissipations, GLs, heater and cooler logic; solved in time.
  • Tools: Thermal Desktop / SINDA, ESATAN-TMS, Siemens NX/Simcenter; FEM is used when thermo-elastic results must feed structures.

Week 9 · Session 9.2

Testing: reduce the uncertainty, then correlate

The tests

  • Thermal balance (TBT): steady hot and cold cases to check and correlate the model.
  • Thermal vacuum (TVT): cycles in vacuum between acceptance or qualification limits, unit powered and working.
  • Thermal cycling (TCT): many cycles, often at ambient pressure, for workmanship and fatigue.
  • Bake-out: heat in vacuum to drive off volatiles before flight.

The chamber

  • ≈ 10−6 mbar (like 200 km altitude) removes gas conduction: the Knudsen number, mean free path over gap size, exceeds ≈ 3.
  • Rough pumps first, then turbo, cryo or ion pumps for high vacuum.
  • A black, LN₂-cooled copper shroud plays deep space; a solar simulator or IR heaters play the Sun (needs known α).
  • Sensors: thermistors and PRTs for flight; thermocouples for test only; silicon diodes below 60 K.

What happens to the spacecraft in TVAC

  • Outgassing · thermal expansion · thermal cycling · realistic radiative heat transfer.
Student question: "Isn't outgassing something we want to prevent?" Yes, in orbit. That is exactly why we provoke it on the ground: the bake-out drives volatiles out in the chamber, where a cold plate and TQCM catch them, instead of letting them condense on optics or detectors in flight.
"Why not just improve the chamber's insulation?" The chamber's job is to reproduce the flight environment around the test article. Better chamber insulation cannot tell us how the spacecraft's own materials and joints behave; only testing the flight hardware can.

At KFUPM: KFUPM has its own spacecraft thermal-vacuum chamber, sized for small-satellite TVT and bake-out.

How much vacuum is enough? Mean free path and the Knudsen number

N₂ at 300 K, molecular diameter d ≈ 0.37 nm. Rough pumps take the chamber to ≈ 10−3 mbar; turbomolecular, cryo or ion pumps take it below 10−6 mbar.

λ = kBT / (√2 π d² P)   ·   Kn = λ / L
Mean free path λ
Knudsen number
Gas conduction regime
Kn > 3: free-molecular. Molecules cross the gap without colliding, and heat transfer is proportional to pressure, so it vanishes as P falls. Kn < 0.3: viscous. Heat moves by collisions and is independent of pressure. Test chambers run near 10−6 mbar (≈ 200 km altitude), where λ ≈ 70 m and residual-gas conduction is negligible.

Parker Solar Probe: a thermal design you can follow step by step

Launched 12 August 2018. 24 orbits over seven years. Final perihelia at 9.86 solar radii, 6.9 million km from the Sun's surface, at ≈ 690 000 km/h. We will redo the key thermal analyses with the tools from this week.

Parker Solar Probe · Step 1

Requirements and environment: what the design has to survive

Thermal requirements

Orbit drives everything

  • Seven Venus flybys lower perihelion from 35.7 R☉ (orbits 1–3) to 9.86 R☉ (orbits 22–24).
  • Aphelion is near Venus's orbit (≈ 0.73 AU): the same spacecraft sees a factor of ≈ 250 in solar flux every orbit.

Work it out: the flux at 9.86 R☉

  1. d = 9.86 × 695 700 km = 6.86 × 106 km = 0.0459 AU
  2. q = 1361 / 0.0459² ≈ 647 kW/m² ≈ 475 suns (the mission quotes "more than 480 suns")
  3. Sun's angular radius: asin(1/9.86) = 5.8°, against 0.27° at Earth. Close in, the Sun is a disc, not a point.
Hot but not heat. The corona is above a million kelvin, but its density is so low that particles deliver little energy. The design case is sunlight, which is why a reflective shield works at all.
Parker Solar Probe · Step 2 · Worked example

The heat shield: one surface takes the Sun so nothing else has to

Design choices

  • Architecture: a 2.4 m (8 ft) disc, 11.4 cm (4.5 in) thick, ≈ 73 kg (160 lb), always pointed at the Sun inside 0.7 AU.
  • Faces: carbon-carbon composite sheets under 2.5 mm thick: strong at 1400 °C.
  • Core: carbon foam, 97 % air: very light and a poor conductor.
  • Coating: white aluminium oxide on the Sun side, with a hair-thin tungsten layer so alumina and carbon cannot react. Porous on purpose so it does not crack.
  • Mount: six attachment points on a welded truss, to limit conduction into the bus.

Find

What α/ε does the front need to stay near 1370 °C? How much heat leaks through?

  1. Front face, back insulated, radiating only forward: α q = ε σ Tf4
  2. A black front (α = ε) would reach (647 000/σ)1/4 = 1838 K (1565 °C)
  3. For Tf = 1644 K (2500 °F): α/ε = σTf4/q = 414/647 ≈ 0.64. The white coating buys ≈ 200 K.
  4. Back face ≈ 315 °C (600 °F) = 589 K radiates σTb4 ≈ 6.8 kW/m² at most: ≈ 1 % of the incident flux.
  5. Implied foam conductance: keff ≈ qbL/ΔT = 6810 × 0.114 / 1055 ≈ 0.7 W/m·K (conduction plus radiation through the pores)
Answer: α/ε ≈ 0.64 and ≈ 99 % of the load is re-radiated from the front.

Simplified one-dimensional estimate for teaching; ε changes with temperature (Session 9.1).

Parker Solar Probe · Step 3

Living in the shadow: umbra geometry and autonomous pointing

Geometry

  1. Because the Sun is a disc of angular radius θ, the fully shadowed umbra is a cone behind the shield, not a cylinder.
  2. Umbra length: Lu = Rshield/tan θ = 1.2/tan 5.8° ≈ 11.8 m. At 1 AU it would be ≈ 260 m.
  3. Shadow radius 3 m behind the shield: 1.2 − 3 tan 5.8° ≈ 0.89 m. Everything (bus, radiators, retracted arrays) must fit inside.
  4. A pointing error δ shifts the shadow by z tan δ. With a 0.5 m bus half-width at 3 m, the budget is ≈ 7°.
Autonomy. Light takes ≈ 8 min each way, far too slow for ground control. Small light sensors on the shield's edge detect sunlight spilling past it, and the flight computer corrects attitude by itself.

Parker Solar Probe · Step 4

Solar arrays: the part that cannot hide

Design choices

  • Retract, don't expose. Near the Sun the wings fold back into the shadow, leaving only a sliver at a grazing angle. Wing angle controls how much light (and heat) they take.
  • Actively cool what's left. The Solar Array Cooling System pumps about one gallon (≈ 3.7 L) of deionized water through the array platens to four radiators behind the shield.
  • Peak heat load at 9.86 R☉: ≈ 5 900 W across the two platens.
  • Platens are held near 60–65 °C by wing-angle control; the water loop spans only ≈ 10 °C end to end.

Why water, not ammonia?

The loop must run up to ≈ 150 °C. Ammonia's critical point is 132 °C, so above it there is no liquid to pump. Pressurised water stays liquid to ≈ 210 °C and has a very high specific heat. Its weakness is freezing at 0 °C.

Work it out: how much radiator?

  1. A = Q / (εσT4) with Q = 5900 W, ε = 0.85, clear view to space
  2. At 100 °C (373 K): A ≈ 6.3 m². At 150 °C (423 K): A ≈ 3.8 m²
  3. Running hotter shrinks the radiator by T⁴: one reason the loop is designed to run warm.

Solving the freezing problem

  • Water kept at 45–50 °C by ground heaters before launch, stored in a heated accumulator.
  • The loop launched dry; water was released ≈ 90 minutes after launch, once the arrays were deployed and producing heat.
  • Only two radiators were opened at first; the other two 33 days later, when the heat load had grown.
Parker Solar Probe · Step 5

Layered defence: from 475 suns to a 30 °C instrument deck

Inside the bus

  • A barrier blanket behind the shield: ≈ 165–170 °C measured at 35.7 R☉, ≈ 250 °C predicted at 9.86 R☉.
  • Core bus held at +10 to +50 °C, largely independent of solar distance once in umbra attitude.
  • Battery isolated from the bus, 0 to +15 °C, with a 10.5 W heater. Worst-case duty 62 %: inside the ≤ 70 % heater rule from the sizing slides.

The instrument that looks at the Sun

  • Solar Probe Cup peeks around the shield to catch solar-wind particles.
  • Built from titanium-zirconium-molybdenum (melts ≈ 2350 °C), tungsten grids (≈ 3420 °C), niobium wiring, and wires suspended in sapphire tubes.
  • Material choice replaces thermal control where shading is impossible.
Parker Solar Probe · Step 6

Verification: testing what cannot be tested whole

  • No chamber can reproduce 475 suns over a 2.4 m shield. So the problem was split: materials at full flux on small samples, the system at lower flux with simulators.
  • Shield coating: samples heated with a high-temperature plasma-arc lamp at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, inside a vacuum chamber (carbon foam would burn in air). Qualified to ≈ 1650 °C.
  • Cooling system: integrated thermal-vacuum test in March 2017 calibrated the SACS across its operating range.
  • Whole spacecraft: thermal-vacuum test in early 2018 with a TPS simulator driven to 325 °C and the SACS to 125 °C.

Putting it together: New Horizons, from 1 AU to Pluto

One spacecraft had to survive near-Earth sunlight at launch and ≈ 1/1000 of it at Pluto, on under 250 W of RTG power. The design is a thermos bottle.

  • MLI blankets the whole bus, including the back of the high-gain antenna.
  • Louvers open near Earth and close in the outer solar system.
  • RTG on a thermally isolating mount with a thermal shield; its waste heat is kept outside.
  • Shunt panel converts surplus electrical power into heat inside the bus.
  • Dedicated radiators for the LORRI camera, star trackers and RF switches; trim radiators for the bus.
  • Thermally coupled propellant tank and electronics so they share heat.
