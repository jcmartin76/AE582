1. Why We Go to Space — Satellites, Orbits & Velocity

An orbit is a regular, repeating path that one body takes around another under gravity, and a satellite is simply any body in orbit — natural (the Moon) or artificial (a spacecraft). We go to space for the vantage it gives: a global view for communications, navigation, weather and Earth observation; an undisturbed platform for astronomy above the atmosphere; access to micro-gravity; and the reach to explore other worlds.

The crucial insight — Newton's — is that orbiting is just falling and continually missing the ground. Imagine a cannon on a very tall mountain firing horizontally. Fire slowly and the ball arcs down and hits the Earth. Fire faster and it lands farther away. Fire fast enough and the curve of its fall exactly matches the curvature of the Earth: it falls forever without getting closer — it is in orbit. Getting to space is therefore not mainly about going up; it is about going sideways fast enough. Altitude is cheap; velocity is the real challenge, and everything in launch and mission design flows from that fact.

2. The Velocities That Define Spaceflight

Three speeds separate the three families of space vehicles: the speed to orbit the Earth (~7.9 km/s), the speed to escape it (~11.2 km/s), and the still-higher energies needed for interplanetary trajectories. Let us derive the first two from Newton's law of gravity.

2.1 Circular orbital velocity

For a circular orbit, gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force needed to bend the path into a circle. Setting the two equal gives the orbital speed:

$$v_{circ}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}},\qquad \mu=GM_\oplus=3.986\times10^{5}\ \text{km}^3/\text{s}^2$$
— circular orbital velocity

Gravity on a satellite of mass $m$ at radius $r$ is $F_g=\dfrac{GM_\oplus m}{r^2}=\dfrac{\mu m}{r^2}$. For a circular orbit this must equal the centripetal force $F_c=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$:

$$\frac{\mu m}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}\;\Rightarrow\; v^2=\frac{\mu}{r}\;\Rightarrow\; v_{circ}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}.$$

The mass $m$ cancels — all satellites at the same altitude orbit at the same speed. Lower orbits (smaller $r$) are faster: this is why LEO satellites whip around in ~90 minutes while GEO takes 24 hours.

2.2 Orbital period — Kepler's third law

$$T=\frac{2\pi r}{v}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}\qquad(\text{circular}),\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}\ \ (\text{general})$$
— the period from the speed

The satellite travels one circumference $2\pi r$ per revolution at speed $v_{circ}=\sqrt{\mu/r}$, so

$$T=\frac{2\pi r}{v_{circ}}=\frac{2\pi r}{\sqrt{\mu/r}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}.$$

For an ellipse the same result holds with the semi-major axis $a$ in place of $r$ — this is Kepler's third law, $T^2\propto a^3$.

2.3 Escape velocity

To leave the Earth entirely, a vehicle needs enough kinetic energy to climb out of the gravitational well and just barely reach infinity with zero speed left over:

$$v_{esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}=\sqrt{2}\;v_{circ}$$
— escape velocity is $\sqrt2$ × circular

The specific mechanical energy is $\varepsilon=\dfrac12 v^2-\dfrac{\mu}{r}$. To "just escape," the vehicle reaches $r\to\infty$ with $v\to0$, i.e. $\varepsilon=0$:

$$\frac12 v_{esc}^2-\frac{\mu}{r}=0\;\Rightarrow\; v_{esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}=\sqrt2\,\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_{circ}.$$

So escape needs only about 41% more speed than a circular orbit at the same radius — 11.2 km/s versus 7.9 km/s at the Earth's surface.

2.4 The rocket equation — why velocity is so hard to buy

Why is reaching 7.9 km/s the whole game? Because a rocket must carry and throw out its own propellant, and the price of velocity is exponential in mass. Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation relates the velocity change $\Delta v$ to the exhaust speed $v_e$ and the mass ratio:

$$\Delta v = v_e\,\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m_f}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{m_0}{m_f}=e^{\,\Delta v/v_e}$$
— Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation

In free space, conservation of momentum for a rocket of instantaneous mass $m$ ejecting propellant at exhaust speed $v_e$ gives $m\,dv=-v_e\,dm$. Rearranging and integrating from initial mass $m_0$ to final (burnout) mass $m_f$:

$$\int_0^{\Delta v}dv=-v_e\int_{m_0}^{m_f}\frac{dm}{m}\;\Rightarrow\; \Delta v=v_e\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}.$$

The dependence is logarithmic in mass but the mass ratio is exponential in the required $\Delta v$. With $v_e\approx 3$ km/s (a good chemical engine) and $\Delta v\approx 9.4$ km/s to reach LEO (orbital speed plus gravity and drag losses), $m_0/m_f=e^{3.1}\approx 23$ — the vehicle must be roughly 95% propellant. That is why launch vehicles are giant tanks, why we stage them, and why every kilogram of spacecraft mass is fought over.

The one-sentence takeaway of Week 1: what makes spaceflight hard is not distance but velocity — and because the rocket equation makes velocity cost exponential propellant, mass is the currency of the entire enterprise.

3. Orbit Regimes & Mission Design

Once in orbit, the shape and size of that orbit are chosen to serve the mission. The master equation relating speed to position on any orbit is the vis-viva equation, which follows from energy conservation:

$$v=\sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right)}$$
— the vis-viva equation

The specific orbital energy is constant and, for a bound orbit of semi-major axis $a$, equals $\varepsilon=-\dfrac{\mu}{2a}$. Writing $\varepsilon$ also as kinetic plus potential energy per unit mass:

$$\frac12 v^2-\frac{\mu}{r}=-\frac{\mu}{2a}\;\Rightarrow\; v^2=\mu\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right).$$

Special cases fall straight out: a circle ($a=r$) gives $v=\sqrt{\mu/r}$; the escape limit ($a\to\infty$) gives $v=\sqrt{2\mu/r}$. On an ellipse, $v$ is largest at perigee (small $r$) and smallest at apogee — exactly what the demo shows.

3.2 Why GEO sits at 35 786 km

The geostationary altitude is not arbitrary — it is exactly the radius whose orbital period equals one Earth rotation, so the satellite keeps pace with the ground beneath it.

$$r_{GEO}=\left(\frac{\mu T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}=42\,164\ \text{km}\;\Rightarrow\; h_{GEO}=r_{GEO}-R_\oplus\approx 35\,786\ \text{km}$$
— solving Kepler's third law for GEO

Set the period equal to one sidereal day, $T=86\,164$ s, and invert $T=2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu}$:

$$r=\left(\frac{\mu T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}=\left(\frac{(3.986\times10^{5})(86\,164)^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}\approx 42\,164\ \text{km}.$$

Subtracting $R_\oplus=6378$ km gives the familiar $35\,786$ km altitude. (Using the sidereal day, not the 24-h solar day, is what makes the satellite truly track the stars-fixed rotation of the Earth.)

4. The Total System — Space & Ground Segments

A "satellite mission" is never just the satellite. It is a system with two halves that must be designed together:

  • The space segment — the spacecraft itself (payload + bus), and often several of them in a constellation.
  • The ground segment — the ground stations that send commands and receive data, the mission-operations centre that flies the spacecraft, and the data-processing and distribution chain that turns raw downlink into a usable product for the end user.

Linking them is the data flow: the payload senses; the bus points, powers and stores; the communications subsystem downlinks to a ground station in view; operations schedules contacts and uploads commands; and processing delivers the science or service. Coverage gaps, contact times, downlink volume and latency are all system-level trades — a decision about the orbit (§3) ripples straight into how many ground stations you need and how quickly a user gets their data.

5. Anatomy of a Spacecraft — Payload & Bus

Every spacecraft divides into two parts. The payload is the reason the mission exists — the camera, radar, transponder or science instrument. The bus (or service module) is everything that exists to keep the payload alive, aimed and connected. A useful way to remember the bus's job is the seven things a spacecraft must do for its payload:

  • Point it — aim the payload accurately (attitude determination & control).
  • Power it — generate, store and distribute electrical power (solar arrays, batteries).
  • Talk for it — send its data down and receive commands (communications / TT&C).
  • Keep it at temperature — reject or retain heat (thermal control).
  • Hold it together — carry launch and on-orbit loads (structure & mechanisms).
  • Move it — change or maintain the orbit and dump momentum (propulsion).
  • Run it — command, schedule, store and process data (command & data handling, C&DH).

These map onto the bus subsystems, which are tightly coupled — a change in one ripples into the others (a bigger payload needs more power → bigger arrays and batteries → more mass → more structure and propellant → a bigger launch mass):

SubsystemJob
Structure & mechanismsThe backbone that carries launch loads and mounts everything; deploys arrays and antennas.
Thermal controlKeeps every unit within its temperature limits using radiators, heaters, coatings and insulation.
Electrical power (EPS)Generates (solar arrays), stores (batteries) and regulates/distributes power.
Attitude determination & control (ADCS)Senses orientation (star trackers, gyros, Sun sensors) and controls it (reaction wheels, thrusters, magnetorquers).
PropulsionProvides $\Delta v$ for orbit insertion, maintenance and disposal, and momentum management.
Communications (TT&C)Telemetry down, commands up; and the payload data downlink.

QUICK CHECK
Which statement best captures the payload-vs-bus split?
Answer: B. The payload delivers the mission (the "why"); the bus provides the seven services — point, power, talk, cool, hold, move, run — that let the payload do its job. The two are tightly coupled: sizing the payload sizes the whole bus.

6. Systems Engineering — Requirements, Budgets & Margins

Systems engineering is the discipline that keeps all those coupled subsystems pulling in the same direction. It starts by turning what the mission must achieve into what each subsystem must do, and it keeps everyone honest with budgets.

6.1 Requirements flow-down and traceability

High-level mission objectives (shaped by user needs and political/financial constraints) become mission requirements, which flow down into system and then subsystem requirements, and finally into component specifications. Two tables keep this chain traceable:

  • The Science (or System) Traceability Matrix (STM) — the "why": it traces top-level goals down to the measurements and the hardware needed to achieve them, so every instrument earns its place.
  • The Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) — the "what/how-verified": it links every requirement to its parent and to the test or analysis that will verify it, so nothing is orphaned or unproven.
Why traceability matters: flow-down prevents "requirements creep" and gold-plating — if a requirement can't be traced up to a mission objective, it shouldn't drive the design; and if it can't be traced down to a verification, you can't prove you met it.

6.3 Budgets and margins

Everyone competes for the same finite resources, so the systems engineer tracks them in budgets. The two most important are:

  • Mass budget: every component's mass rolls up to the launch mass the rocket must lift. Because the rocket equation makes mass so precious (§2.4), this is often the binding constraint.
  • Power budget: generation (arrays) versus consumption (all loads), across every mission mode and worst-case eclipse — the batteries must carry the spacecraft through the dark.

Because early estimates are uncertain, budgets carry margin — a deliberate reserve (often ~20% early in a program) that shrinks as the design matures and masses are actually measured. A budget with no margin is a budget that will be blown.

$$m_{dry}=m_{payload}+m_{bus},\qquad m_{launch}=m_{dry}\,(1+\text{margin})+m_{propellant}$$

Worked Problem 5 — Why mass is the currency (rocket equation)
A stage needs $\Delta v=3.0$ km/s with an engine of exhaust speed $v_e=3.0$ km/s. What fraction of the initial mass must be propellant?
$\dfrac{m_0}{m_f}=e^{\Delta v/v_e}=e^{1.0}=2.72$. So $m_f=m_0/2.72=0.368\,m_0$, and the propellant is $m_0-m_f=$ $0.63\,m_0$ — about 63% of the launch mass. Ask for $\Delta v=9.4$ km/s to LEO and the ratio balloons to $e^{3.1}\approx 23$ (~95% propellant) — the exponential reason launchers are giant tanks and spacecraft dry mass is fought over kilogram by kilogram.

7. Reliability & the Mission Lifecycle

You cannot send a technician to orbit. A spacecraft must work first time and keep working for years, so reliability is engineered in from the start:

  • Redundancy on the most critical, hardest-to-replace functions (dual strings, cross-strapping), so a single failure is not fatal — spent where it buys the most survival per kilogram.
  • Space qualification — parts and assemblies are tested to survive launch (vibration, shock, acoustics) and the space environment (vacuum, thermal cycling, radiation) with margin, before they ever fly.
  • Simplicity and heritage — proven designs and fewer moving parts fail less often.

All of this is organised by the mission lifecycle, a sequence of phases with reviews (gates) between them. In the ESA/NASA scheme:

PhaseWhat happens
Phase 0 / AMission analysis and feasibility; concept and requirements — is it worth doing, and roughly how?
Phase BPreliminary definition — the design is baselined (PDR).
Phase CDetailed design — everything is specified for build (CDR).
Phase DProduction, assembly, integration and test (AIT/AIV), then launch.
Phase EOperations — flying the mission and returning the data (and, at end of life, disposal).
The course project — a CubeSat — walks this same path in miniature: objectives → requirements → subsystem design → budgets → build and test.

1. Two satellites orbit at the same altitude but one is twice as massive. How do their orbital speeds compare?
Answer: C. In $v=\sqrt{\mu/r}$ the satellite mass has cancelled out. At a given radius every object orbits at the same speed — a heavy telescope and a paint fleck keep pace.
2. Escape velocity from a given radius is larger than the circular-orbit velocity there by what factor?
Answer: B. $v_{esc}=\sqrt{2\mu/r}=\sqrt2\,\sqrt{\mu/r}=\sqrt2\,v_{circ}$ — 11.2 vs 7.9 km/s at the Earth's surface.
3. In a first-cut mass budget, the 20% margin is applied to…
Answer: B. The uncertain, still-growing dry mass carries the margin: $160\times1.2=192$ kg, then propellant is added to reach the 217 kg launch mass. Load the lecture preset in Interactive 2 to see it.
4. A mission needs continuous global navigation with only a couple of dozen satellites. Which regime fits best?
Answer: B — MEO. GPS and Galileo fly near 20 000 km: high enough that ~24–30 satellites cover the whole globe continuously, yet far cheaper in count than a LEO mega-constellation. A single GEO can't provide the geometry navigation needs.
5. Why is the ballistic penalty of extra spacecraft mass so severe?
Answer: B. From $m_0/m_f=e^{\Delta v/v_e}$, buying velocity costs exponentially in mass. Every extra kilogram of spacecraft must be lifted by a much larger mass of propellant (and tankage) — which is exactly why the mass budget is the systems engineer's tightest leash.
