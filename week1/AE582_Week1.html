Week 1 · Session 1.1
Why engineer in space — and what is a satellite?

An orbit and a satellite

An orbit is a regular, repeating path one body takes around another under gravity. A satellite is any body in orbit — natural (the Moon) or artificial (a spacecraft).

Why go?

  • Global view — comms, navigation, weather, Earth observation
  • A clear platform above the atmosphere — astronomy
  • Micro-gravity, and the reach to explore other worlds
Week 1 · Session 1.1
The challenge isn't going up — it's going sideways fast
  • Newton's cannonball: fire horizontally fast enough and the ball's fall curves to match the Earth's curvature — it falls forever without landing. That is an orbit.
  • Orbiting is falling while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground.
  • Altitude is comparatively cheap; reaching orbital velocity is the real challenge — and it sets everything about launch and mission design.

Week 1 · Session 1.1
The equations behind those speeds
Circular speed $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{\mu}{r}}$
Period $T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{a^3}{\mu}}$
Escape $v_{esc}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\mu}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_{circ}$
Vis-viva $v=\sqrt{\mu\!\left(\dfrac{2}{r}-\dfrac{1}{a}\right)}$
Gravity = centripetal force gives $v=\sqrt{\mu/r}$; energy conservation gives escape and vis-viva. $\mu=GM_\oplus=3.986\times10^5$ km³/s².
Week 1 · Session 1.1
Why velocity is so hard to buy — the rocket equation
$\Delta v = v_e\,\ln\dfrac{m_0}{m_f}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{m_0}{m_f}=e^{\Delta v/v_e}$
  • The mass ratio grows exponentially with the velocity you need.
  • To reach LEO ($\Delta v\approx 9.4$ km/s) with $v_e\approx 3$ km/s → $m_0/m_f\approx 23$: the vehicle is ~95% propellant.
  • This is why rockets are giant tanks, why we stage them — and why mass is the currency of the whole enterprise.

Week 1 · Session 1.1
The total system — space and ground segments

Space segment

  • The spacecraft: payload + bus
  • Often several, in a constellation

Ground segment

  • Ground stations — commands up, data down
  • Mission operations — flying the spacecraft
  • Processing & distribution — raw downlink → user product
Contact times, downlink volume and latency are system trades — the orbit choice ripples straight into the ground segment.
Week 1 · Session 1.1 · worked example
Circular-orbit speed and period

Given

Circular orbit, $h=500$ km, $R_\oplus=6378$ km, $\mu=398\,600$ km³/s².

Find

Orbital speed $v$ and period $T$.

Solution

$r=R_\oplus+h=6878$ km
$v=\sqrt{\mu/r}=\sqrt{398600/6878}\approx\mathbf{7.61}$ km/s
$T=2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu}\approx 5677$ s $\approx\mathbf{94.6}$ min

Week 1 · Session 1 · recap
Key takeaways
  • A satellite is any body in orbit; what makes spaceflight hard is velocity (7.9 km/s to orbit, 11.2 to escape).
  • The rocket equation makes velocity cost exponential propellant — mass is the currency.
  • Mission type dictates the orbit — coverage, altitude, inclination and revisit.
  • A mission is a total system: space segment + ground segment, designed together.

Traceability — the STM and the RTM

STM — the "why"

The Science Traceability Matrix traces top-level goals down to the measurements and hardware needed — so every instrument earns its place.

RTM — the "verified"

The Requirements Traceability Matrix links every requirement to its parent and to the test that verifies it — so nothing is orphaned or unproven.

Week 1 · Session 1.2
Anatomy of a spacecraft: payload + bus

Payload

The reason the mission exists — camera, radar, transponder or science instrument.

Bus (service module)

Everything that keeps the payload alive, aimed and connected — a set of tightly-coupled subsystems.

Tightly coupled: a bigger payload needs more power → bigger arrays & batteries → more mass → more structure & propellant → bigger launch mass.
Week 1 · Session 1.2
Seven things the spacecraft must do for its payload
  • Point it — aim accurately (ADCS)
  • Power it — generate, store, distribute (EPS)
  • Talk for it — data down, commands up (comms/TT&C)
  • Keep it at temperature — thermal control
  • Hold it together — carry loads (structure)
  • Move it — orbit & momentum (propulsion)
  • Run it — command, store, process (C&DH)

Week 1 · Session 1.2
Mass and power budgets — and margins

Mass budget

Every component's mass rolls up to the launch mass the rocket must lift — often the binding constraint.

Power budget

Generation vs. consumption across every mode and worst-case eclipse — batteries must carry the dark.

$m_{launch}=(m_{payload}+m_{bus})(1+\text{margin})+m_{propellant}$
Early estimates are uncertain, so budgets carry margin (~20% early), shrinking as the design matures.

Week 1 · Session 1.2 · worked example
A first-cut mass budget

Given

Payload 40 kg · Bus (dry) 120 kg · Propellant 25 kg · Margin 20%.

Solution

Dry mass $=40+120=\mathbf{160}$ kg
With margin $=160\times1.20=\mathbf{192}$ kg
Launch (wet) mass $=192+25=\mathbf{217}$ kg

Propellant mass fraction ≈ 25/217 ≈ 11.5%. Try it live in the previous slide's builder.
Week 1 · Session 1.2
Reliability, redundancy & space qualification
  • No repairs on orbit — the design must work first time, for years.
  • Redundancy on the most critical, hardest-to-replace functions (dual strings, cross-strapping) — spent where it buys the most survival per kilogram.
  • Space qualification — tested to survive launch (vibration, shock) and the environment (vacuum, thermal cycling, radiation) with margin, before flight.
