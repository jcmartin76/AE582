Water-vapor rocket to LEO — a worked solution
The opening design challenge: place a 30 kg satellite into a 450 km circular orbit using a water-vapor rocket. We work it as engineers: first extract the requirements from a mission analysis, then read what the rocket equation actually demands, build the case for staging, deliver a feasibility verdict — and close with a spin-off: can water vapor work as an orbital-maneuvering thruster?

The problem
How a designer attacks this
We never start from the hardware. We start by turning the mission into numerical requirements, then test any propulsion choice against them.
Requirement R0 Deliver a payload of 30 kg to the target orbit. (given)
Plan:  (1) mission analysis → derive the speed and ΔV requirements  ·  (2) the propellant → what water vapor can offer  ·  (3) the rocket equation → the mass it demands  ·  (4) staging → can it help?  ·  (5) verdict  ·  (6) spin-off: on-orbit use.
Requirements
Deriving R1 — the orbital speed
A circular orbit is a balance between gravity and inertia. The speed that holds a 450 km orbit follows from setting gravity equal to the centripetal requirement:
$$\frac{\mu}{r^2}=\frac{v_c^2}{r}\;\Rightarrow\;v_c=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}},\quad r=R_\oplus+h=6828\text{ km}$$
Requirement R1 The payload must be accelerated to an orbital speed of 7.64 km/s (period 93.6 min) — this is a speed target, not an altitude target.
Requirements
Deriving R2 — the velocity budget ΔV
A launcher cannot simply “have” 7.64 km/s — it must build that speed while fighting gravity and air. Each effect is a term we add to the budget:

Requirement R2 The vehicle must supply a total velocity budget of ΔV ≈ 9.4 km/s. This is the number the rocket equation must be paid in propellant.
Propellant
What water vapor can offer
Store water; add heat so it flashes to steam; expand the vapor through a nozzle. No combustion, no pumps, no ignition — a “warm-gas” (resistojet-class) thruster.
  • Exhaust speed scales as $v_e\propto\sqrt{T_C/M_W}$. Water is only mildly heavy ($M_W=18$), but with no combustion the chamber is cool (heater-limited, a few hundred K), so $v_e$ stays small.
  • Typical performance: $I_{sp}\approx110$ s ⇒ effective exhaust velocity $v_{eq}=I_{sp}\,g_0=\mathbf{1079\ m/s}$.
  • For comparison, chemical combustion ($T_C\approx3000$–3600 K) gives $I_{sp}=300$–450 s, i.e. $v_{eq}=3$–4.4 km/s.
Capability Water vapor delivers about $v_{eq}=1.08$ km/s. Set this beside R2 (ΔV = 9.4 km/s) — the whole problem lives in that mismatch.
Rocket eq.
The rocket equation — the idea before the algebra
A rocket speeds up by throwing mass (propellant) out the back. The catch: the propellant it will burn a minute from now must be carried — and accelerated — right now. Early propellant has to push later propellant, so the cost of speed compounds. That compounding is what the equation captures.
$$\Delta V \;=\; v_{eq}\,\ln\!\frac{M_0}{M_f}$$
  • $M_0$ — wet mass: everything at ignition (payload + structure + propellant).
  • $M_f$ — dry mass: what is left when the tanks run dry (payload + structure).
  • $v_{eq}=I_{sp}g_0$ — the exchange rate: how much velocity you buy per unit of propellant thrown.
  • $\ln(M_0/M_f)$ — the logarithm is the fingerprint of compounding: doubling your speed does not double your propellant, it squares your mass ratio.
Rocket eq.
Reading the exponential — why $v_{eq}$ is everything
Invert the equation to see the price of speed directly:
$$\frac{M_0}{M_f}=\Lambda=e^{\,\Delta V/v_{eq}}$$
The only thing that matters is the ratio $\Delta V/v_{eq}$ — the mission’s demand measured in units of the engine’s exhaust speed. Each whole unit multiplies your starting mass by $e\approx2.72$:

Rocket eq.
Our numbers — what water vapor is being asked to do
1
Measure the demand in exhaust-speed units
$\dfrac{\Delta V}{v_{eq}}=\dfrac{9400}{1079}=8.7$. From the table above, anything past ~3 is already hopeless — we are at 8.7.
2
Turn it into a mass ratio
$\Lambda=e^{8.7}\approx\mathbf{6100}$. In words: you need about 6100 kg of vehicle at ignition for every 1 kg you want in orbit.
3
Best-case propellant (pretend the structure is weightless)
$M_p=m_{pl}(\Lambda-1)=30\times6099\approx\mathbf{183\ tonnes}$ of water to lift a 30 kg satellite.
4
Add a real tank ($\varepsilon_s=0.15$)
Feasibility needs $1/\Lambda>\varepsilon_s$; here $1/\Lambda=1.6\times10^{-4}\lll0.15$. The empty tank alone outweighs the budget — payload fraction is negative.
A single water-vapor stage cannot reach LEO — at any payload.
Staging
Why even a great single stage hits a wall
Suppose we had a much better propellant. There is still a hard ceiling on what one stage can do, because it must drag its empty tank all the way to burnout. Setting payload to zero in the rocket equation gives that ceiling:
$$\Delta V_{\max}^{\text{(1 stage)}}=v_{eq}\,\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon_s},\qquad \varepsilon_s=\frac{M_{\text{structure}}}{M_{\text{structure}}+M_{\text{prop}}}$$
  • Even a fine kerolox stage ($v_{eq}=3.33$ km/s, $\varepsilon_s=0.10$) tops out at $\Delta V_{\max}=3.33\ln 10=\mathbf{7.7\ km/s}$ — still short of the 9.4 km/s in R2, and that is with zero payload.
  • Why: near burnout you are spending precious $\Delta V$ accelerating an almost-empty tank. The dead structure caps you.
So one stage fails R2 even for chemistry. This is the motivation for staging.
Staging
The idea of a multi-stage rocket
If the empty tank is the problem, throw it away. Build the vehicle as a stack of stages; when a stage burns out, jettison its dead structure and light the next one.

What you gain

You stop accelerating spent tanks and engines. The next stage begins with a small dry mass, so its mass ratio is favourable again — a fresh start on the exponential.

How it adds up

Velocities add across stages, but the payload fractions multiply: $$\Delta V=\textstyle\sum_i \Delta V_i,\qquad \pi=\prod_i \pi_i.$$
Splitting the total $\Delta V$ across $n$ stages keeps each stage’s $\Delta V_i/v_{eq}$ small — so each mass ratio stays modest, and their product can remain a usable payload.
▶ INTERACTIVEStaging explorer — hold $\Delta V$ at 9.4 km/s and add stages; watch a negative payload turn positive
Staging
The case for multi-stage — it rescues a chemical launcher
1
One kerolox stage
Ceiling 7.7 km/s $<$ 9.4 km/s → infeasible, at any payload.
2
Split into two equal stages
Each does $\Delta V=4.7$ km/s: $\Lambda_i=e^{4700/3334}=4.09$, so $\pi_i=\dfrac{1/\Lambda_i-\varepsilon_s}{1-\varepsilon_s}=16.0\%$.
3
Multiply the stages
$\pi=0.160\times0.160=2.6\%$ → 30 kg rides on a ~1.17 tonne two-stage vehicle. Feasible.
Staging turns an impossible single-stage mission into a real one — this is why launchers stage.
Staging
…but can staging rescue water vapor?
Staging is the right tool — so let us honestly apply it to water vapor.
$$\Delta V_{\max}^{\text{(vapor stage)}}=1079\,\ln\frac{1}{0.15}=2.05\text{ km/s per stage (zero payload)}$$
  • To reach 9.4 km/s you would need $9.4/2.05\approx\mathbf{4.6}$ stages just to move zero payload — and every stage must still carry the entire stack above it, so the masses re-explode.
  • The key distinction: staging cures the structure penalty, not a low $v_{eq}$. Water vapor’s exhaust speed is fundamentally too small, and no amount of staging raises $v_{eq}$.
Multi-stage does not save water vapor — the limit is the propellant itself.

Spin-off
Spin-off: water vapor where ΔV is small
The mission failed because $\Delta V/v_{eq}=8.7$ was enormous. But on orbit, the jobs are tiny — tens to a couple hundred m/s — so that ratio becomes small, and the exponential barely bites.
$$\text{for }x=\tfrac{\Delta V}{v_{eq}}\ll1:\quad \Lambda=e^{x}\approx1+x\;\Rightarrow\; M_p\approx m_{pl}\,\frac{\Delta V}{v_{eq}}$$
The cost becomes almost linear and cheap: a low $v_{eq}$ that was fatal for launch is perfectly acceptable when $\Delta V$ is only a whisper of it. So the honest question is not “can water vapor launch?” but “can water vapor maneuver?”

Spin-off
Can it work as a thruster? Verdict & trade-offs
YES — for small on-orbit ΔV (tens to ~a couple hundred m/s), water vapor is not only feasible but attractive.

Why it wins here

  • Safe & “green” — non-toxic, non-flammable, easy to handle and licence.
  • Dense storage ($\rho=1000$) — small tanks; self-contained, mechanically simple.

What limits it

  • Low $I_{sp}$ caps total $\Delta V$ (fine for station-keeping, not transfers).
  • Needs electrical power/heat to vaporize; low thrust → long burns; must avoid freezing.
This is a real, growing class of smallsat propulsion — steam/resistojet and water-electrolysis thrusters — precisely because on-orbit $\Delta V$ is small. Match the propellant to the job.
Worked solution — the takeaway
Requirements first; the rocket equation decides; match $v_{eq}$ to the job
Mission analysis set the requirements — R1: 7.64 km/s orbital speed; R2: $\Delta V\approx9.4$ km/s. Water vapor offers only $v_{eq}\approx1.08$ km/s, forcing a mass ratio near 6100 that no structure survives, and staging — which cures dead-mass, not low $v_{eq}$ — cannot rescue it. Launch: infeasible. Yet where $\Delta V$ is small, the same propellant shines: a few kilograms of water run a 30 kg satellite’s whole on-orbit life. On-orbit thruster: feasible.
