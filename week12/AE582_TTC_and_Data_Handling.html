PART I · TT&C OVERVIEW

Telemetry and command keep information flowing both ways between spacecraft and ground

Two-way flow. The telemetry, command and data-handling functions ensure the two-way flow of information between the spacecraft and ground control: transmission (downlink) and reception (uplink) of data, and management of payload data.
Design drivers. Mission, orbit, payload type and ground control stations.
Telemetry downlink. Informs the ground about the functioning of the subsystems, to detect any fault and diagnose its cause. It may also carry payload data for scientific or Earth-observation missions.
Command uplink. Lets the ground controller change the role of the spacecraft — reorient it, operate a mechanism, or for other reasons.
Continuous link — GEO
Geostationary satellites such as Intelsat and Inmarsat maintain a continuous link with their ground stations.
Intermittent link — non-GEO
Non-geosynchronous satellites such as Envisat or the ISS need on-board data storage for intermittent contact with any ground station in sight…
…or a relay
The Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System (TDRSS) makes the ground link via special-purpose relay spacecraft in GEO.
Key idea: the orbit decides whether the link is continuous or intermittent — and that decides how much the spacecraft must store and how autonomous it must be.

TT&C performs three functions — command, telemetry and tracking — often with a separate payload data linkfrom [2]

Why a separate PDT link? Scientific instruments can produce far more data than the TM link can carry, so the TT&C is complemented with a dedicated payload data transmission link [2].
Doppler bonus. The same downlink lets the ground station measure the Doppler shift → radial velocity of the spacecraft.

PART I · TT&C OVERVIEW

The TT&C and data-handling subsystem sits at the centre of spacecraft operations

System context, after [1] Fig. 13.1
Central to operations. Allows interaction between spacecraft operators and the system. Commands — typically received via an S-band link — are decoded, queued and distributed to subsystems, with feedback through telemetry, usually from each stage of the command distribution for verification.
Different processing needs. Critical subsystems like deployment have low processing demands; instruments need more processing power. Separate downlinks (X- or Ka-band) carry high-rate payload data.
Ground interface. The system protocol interacts with an on-ground command centre, and sometimes a separate data-collection centre.
Operating modes. From a basic safe mode — the spacecraft kept alive from a command, thermal and power point of view — up to a fully performing platform and payload.

PART I · TT&C OVERVIEW

A basic TT&C subsystem is built for coverage and redundancy; the PDT subsystem is built for ratefrom [2]

Hot redundancy: both receivers always ON, so a TC is received whatever happens.
Cold redundancy: only one transmitter ON; the other is switched in by TC if needed.
Two LGAs, opposite polarizations (LHCP + RHCP) give almost omni-directional coverage in any attitude without destructive interference.
Transponder = TC receiver + TM transmitter + ranging turn-around in one unit; diplexers separate the TC and TM frequencies.
PDT uses a switch, not a hybrid, and an MGA/HGA: every dB of EIRP is needed for the high bit rate.
Ask: why does the TT&C use a 3 dB hybrid (losing 3 dB) while the PDT uses a switch? Because TT&C must work in any attitude and survive failures (coverage and reliability first), whereas the PDT is sized for maximum EIRP towards the ground station.

PART II · TELEMETRY DATA FORMATTING

Telemetry data fall into three classes with very different rates

1 · Housekeeping data

Monitors the health and status of on-board equipment: temperatures of equipment boxes, solar arrays and attitude-control thrusters, pressures in fuel tanks, voltages, currents, operational status, deployment of mechanisms, separation from the launcher.

Hundreds to thousands of parameters — but a low data rate: sampling typically every 30 s to 2 min.
2 · Attitude data

From Sun sensors, Earth sensors and star trackers, gyroscopes and accelerometers.

During certain phases, such as orbital transfers, sampling may be needed up to four times per second — requiring higher bandwidth. For GEO, a reduced rate may be provided.

3 · Payload data

Varies greatly with the mission. Science and Earth-observation missions generate large volumes — often Mbit/s or Gbit/s.

An entirely separate high-rate system may be provided (as on Envisat), and data compression may be used.

Deep space: images may take hours or even days to transmit. Capacity is limited by on-board storage and link bandwidth.
Rule of thumb: housekeeping drives the number of channels, payload drives the data rate.

Telemetry plays a role in every mission phase — but its job changes

Ground testingAccess to inter-unit connections and direct access to avionics data buses allows faster TM/TC rates; RF links are used for performance testing.

PART II · TELEMETRY DATA FORMATTING

Digital data enter the PCM bit stream, which is bi-phase modulated onto a sub-carrier

Digital serial data are often produced in serial form, simplifying wiring. They are stored in shift registers and processed in 8- or 16-bit blocks for integration into the PCM bit stream. Example: data from a subsystem processor stored in RAM before transfer to the telemetry stream.
PCM mode. Data from the ADCs, digital bi-level data and other multiplexed data are loaded into a parallel-to-serial converter and clocked out at the telemetry bit rate as a continuous PCM bit stream. Unique synchronization and identification codes are inserted so data can be identified on the ground. The bit stream is bi-phase modulated onto a coherent sub-carrier before transmission.
Dwell mode. Many encoders can concentrate sampling on one particular word, to the exclusion of other data, by loading the address of the desired channel into the control and timing block.
Biphase-Mark: a transition at the start of every bit; a 1 adds a second transition mid-bit. The signal always has transitions, so the ground can recover the clock, and it has no DC component.
Pulse code modulation (PCM) is a digital scheme for transmitting analogue data: it converts an analogue signal into digital form.

Packet telemetry (CCSDS) replaced fixed PCM formats and improved interoperability

1 · Source packetApplication data wrapped with a packet header: source ID, packet sequence, data length → proper routing and reassembly.
2 · Virtual channelsPackets grouped into virtual channels, placed in fixed-length Virtual Channel Data Units (VCDUs).
3 · Transfer frameVCDUs multiplexed into a single transfer frame for transmission; channels can use different sampling rates for bandwidth efficiency.

Standards: the CCSDS recommendations for Packet Telemetry and Space Packet Protocol, adopted by ESA through the ECSS-E-ST-50 series, replaced traditional PCM telemetry — improving interoperability in projects like the ISS.
Multiplexing (muxing) sends multiple signals or streams over one link at the same time as a single, complex signal.
Demultiplexing directs incoming messages to the appropriate clients based on fields in the protocol headers.

On the link, each TM frame becomes a CADU: sync marker, coding and randomizationfrom [2]

Attached Sync Marker (ASM): a bit pattern that lets the ground find the frame boundaries — the packet-era successor of the PCM sync words.
Randomization: XOR with a pseudo-random sequence (not the ASM) so long runs of 0s or 1s do not break bit synchronisation.
Concatenated coding: RS (outer) + convolutional (inner). With LDPC at CADU level, frames are sliced into blocks, LDPC-encoded and marked with a code sync marker (CSM).

PART II · TELEMETRY DATA FORMATTING

Error-control coding: detect telemetry errors cheaply, correct them when the channel is noisy

Frame loss is not catastrophic for telemetry, so there is no great need for correction of such errors — nevertheless an error-checking code is sometimes included in the frame.
Detection (CRC). On communications spacecraft the downlink BER is usually better than 1 in 10⁵. A simple check: divide the telemetry frame by a polynomial, e.g. x¹⁶ + x¹² + x⁵ + 1, and send the remainder with the data. On the ground the complete frame is divided again: a non-zero result flags an error. Detects, but does not correct, and does not reduce transmission power needs.
Correction. In noisy channels, convolutional coding (inner code) is combined with Reed–Solomon block codes (outer code), improving performance during burst errors.
Example: packet telemetry needs nearly error-free transmission; missions like the Huygens probe to Titan (Saturn system) use such coding to keep power and BER low over long distances.

Agencies standardise the codes: convolutional k = 7, r = 1/2 and Reed–Solomon (255, 223)

Convolutional code: joint NASA/ESA telemetry coding guidelines standardise a code with constraint length k = 7 and rate r = 1/2, widely used across agencies.
Reed–Solomon (255, 223): non-interleaved (I = 1) or interleaved to depth I = 5. 223·I bytes of uncoded information enter the encoder and emerge unaltered with 32·I check bytes appended.
Burst correction: corrects bursts of errors in up to 16·I consecutive bytes — the interleaved form is more effective.

PART II · TELEMETRY DATA FORMATTING

Ground networks, band selection and phase modulation of the downlink

Ground station networks. Spacecraft rely on global networks (like those of NASA and ESA) for telemetry.
Choosing the band: first contact the Frequency Management Office of the networks concerned with a request for the favoured band; second, select discrete frequencies within the allocated band — a very complex process.
Modulation. For telemetry, UHF bands are commonly used, except for Intelsat missions. Phase modulation is universally used for the RF downlink, with PSK-modulated sub-carriers at low bit rates. At higher bit rates, PCM-SPL modulation is preferred for better sideband suppression.
Phase modulation (PM) encodes a message as variations in the instantaneous phase of a carrier wave. With frequency modulation it is one of the two forms of angle modulation.

Small m: the green wave looks almost like the carrier (most power stays in the carrier). Large m: bigger phase jumps carry more data power.

An earth station chains modem, amplifiers, filters and reflector

Modulators, demodulators and modems convert information into a signal that can be transmitted; they use a generic intermediate band.

A link budget is the running sum of every gain and loss between two antennas

Link budget: a summary of all power gains and losses.
Gains are obtained from amplifiers and antennas.
Losses are obtained from cabling, filters and atmospheric propagation — and, above all, free-space path loss.
PRX/N₀ = EIRP + G/T − L − 10log k − α[dBHz]; L = 20 log₁₀(4πsf/c), s = slant range; α = atmospheric, pointing, demodulation losses [2]
▶ Video: link budget walkthrough [3]
Read it like a bank statement: start with transmitter power, add gains, subtract losses — what is left above the noise floor is your SNR, and what is left above the requirement is your margin.

Margins: nominal, mean − 3σ and worst-case RSSfrom [2]

Worst case is inefficient. Traditionally the budget assumed every parameter at its worst value simultaneously — an unlikely event that over-sizes the system.
ECSS approach: compute one budget per scenario (antenna, station, bit rate…). Each parameter has nominal, favourable and adverse values and a probability density function.
Statistical method: by the central limit theorem the SNR is approximately Gaussian with mean μ and variance σ²; worst-case RSS subtracts √(ΣΔA²) from the nominal value.
Rule of thumb [2]: a 3 dB margin for the nominal budget; > 0 dB for both mean − 3σ and worst-case RSS.

Packet telecommand — adopted by ESA and the ECSS standards — consists of several layers that manage the flow of data from the ground station to the spacecraft subsystems.
Application process layer: lets in-orbit payloads or subsystems be controlled remotely by a user — the interface from humans and their computers.
System management layer: translates commands from human-friendly language into detailed communication for the packetization layer.
PART IV · TELECOMMAND

Packet telecommand is a seven-layer stack — click a layer

1 · Application processInterface from humans and computers; remote control of payloads and subsystems.
2 · System managementHuman-friendly commands → detailed communication.
3 · PacketizationTC packets: 48-bit header + variable data field.
4 · SegmentationMultiplex packets into virtual channels; 8-bit segment header.
5 · TransferTransfer frames: 40-bit header, data, 16-bit error control.

7 · PhysicalRF link and its four carrier modulation modes.
On board, in reverse. The receiver, demodulator/command decoder and OBDH operate on the same layers in reverse order. At each stage telemetry is fed back to the ground, confirming good receipt and successful command execution.
Header sizes add up: a short command carries a lot of protocol overhead — the price of routing, sequence control and verification.
PART IV · TELECOMMAND

A memory-load command frame protects every field by repetition and coding

Memory-load command frame structure, after [1] Fig. 13.5
Address/sync word selects the spacecraft and decoder — a command is only executed by the decoder it is addressed to.
Mode word in 2-of-4 code any single bit error produces an invalid word, so a corrupted mode is rejected, never misinterpreted.
Each 12-bit word sent twice (8 data + 4 check bits): single errors corrected, double errors detected; one valid copy is enough.
Telecommand philosophy: it is far better to reject a command (and resend it) than to execute a wrong one.

Asymmetry: error-control encoding is less intensive than decoding. For telemetry this places the complex tasks in the ground segment — but for telecommand the decoder is on board, where electronics are constrained.
BCH code: CCSDS telecommand chose a Bose–Chaudhuri–Hocquenghem (BCH) algorithm whose major strength is error detection. The failure of this code is even used to mark the end of a TC frame.

Inside a transponder: RF down/up-conversion around a digital corefrom [2]

Receive: LNA → down-conversion to IF → ADC → PLL (carrier), Costas loop (sub-carrier), DTTL (symbol timing).
Transmit: digital modulator (CORDIC) → DAC → up-conversion → power amplifier.
Coherent mode: the transmit carrier is derived from the locked uplink × K (turn-around ratio), including the Doppler K·fD.
Without the ranging channel the unit is called a transceiver, not a transponder. The TC data go to the OBDH demodulated but usually not decoded: decoding happens in the data-handling subsystem.
PART V · TRACKING & THE TRANSPONDER

Delta-DOR: two stations and a quasar sharpen deep-space navigationfrom [2]

The gap: range and range-rate give the line-of-sight distance and velocity; the other coordinates come from Earth rotation — a few metres and mm/s, enough for most missions but not for gravity assists, orbit insertions or radio science.
The idea: two distant ground stations receive the spacecraft tones simultaneously; the phase difference gives the angle: φ̂ = cos⁻¹(Δθ·c / (2πfT·dGS)).
Calibration: a quasar within ~0.15 nrad of the spacecraft, observed in the same band, removes station clock and atmosphere errors.
Next: wide-band (PN) DDOR makes the spacecraft signal look like the broadband quasar, reducing instrumental phase dispersion. Used on missions such as Rosetta.

PART VI · COMMUNICATION PROTOCOLS

AOS and Proximity-1 extended CCSDS beyond simple packet telemetry

Advanced Orbiting Systems (AOS)
  • In the 1980s the requirements of the ISS and Earth-observation systems exceeded CCSDS packet telemetry capabilities.
  • Deficiencies: isochronous services (voice/video), high-volume downlinks (EO imagery) and interactive services (two-way fast file transfer).
  • New structure: service-driven rather than protocol/data-format oriented.
  • Introduced networking to CCSDS: the efficient connection-oriented path protocol and support for the Internet Protocol (IP).
Proximity links — CCSDS Proximity-1
  • Designed for power-constrained inter-satellite links, constellation missions and planetary operations (two-way communication).
  • Symmetrical operation: no distinction between telemetry and telecommand, or forward and return links.
  • Mars exploration is a typical example; it uses features adapted from both CCSDS packet telemetry and telecommand.
Automatic link availability detection and parameter negotiation
Reliable in-sequence delivery using automatic retransmission
Convolutional and Reed–Solomon coding in both directions
Supports fixed-length or variable-length frames
PART VI · COMMUNICATION PROTOCOLS

SCPS and CFDP: protocols for networks and files in space

Space Communications Protocol Standards (SCPS)
  • Developed by NASA with the US DoD for military and civil satellite networks.
  • Suite: Network Protocol (SCPS-NP), Security Protocol (SCPS-SP), Transport Protocol (SCPS-TP) and File Protocol (SCPS-FP).
  • Adoption has been slow: it is primarily for complex space constellations.
Think of SCPS as "TCP/IP adapted to long delays, link errors and asymmetric links".
CCSDS File Delivery Protocol (CFDP)
  • Reliable data transfer over CCSDS or other protocols.
  • Store-and-forward networking: an end-to-end path need not exist at the same time.
  • Continuous file delivery supports unbounded telemetry streams.
  • Negative ARQ guarantees delivery, optimised for large bandwidth-delay products and low return-path overhead.
  • Proxy transfer for third-party control of data transfer.
  • Graceful suspend/resume for predictable link outages.
  • Garbage clearance: recognises faults or single-event upsets so systematic errors do not accumulate.
  • File manipulation: remote naming, deletion, copying and management of on-board files.
PART VI · COMMUNICATION PROTOCOLS

The Interplanetary Internet stitches separate internets together with bundles

In-situ InternetsSuch as Earth's Internet or a Mars Internet, supporting common TCP/IP protocols.
Interplanetary BackboneLong-haul transport services using techniques from CFDP.
Interplanetary GatewaysConnect the deployed Internets to each other or to the backbone.
Inter-Internet DialoguesStore-and-forward networking for long delays and disjointed communications from orbital or planetary motion.
The IPN is an initiative of the Internet Research Task Force (IRTF). Inter-internet dialogues use bundling: just as IP moves data across heterogeneous subnets in real time, bundling moves data across heterogeneous internets in a store-and-forward environment.

Classical OBDH: a central processor on a high-integrity serial bus

Role: OBDH systems manage command and data for both telemetry and telecommand.
Payload data: communications spacecraft like Inmarsat handle user data for receipt and transmission; remote-sensing spacecraft manage large instrument volumes — a synthetic aperture radar (SAR) can generate 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps.

PART VII · ON-BOARD DATA HANDLING

Payload data handling and on-board computers are converging with terrestrial practice

PDHT: a Payload Data Handling and Transmission system often manages instrument data at high rates — handling, storing and transmitting it independently via dedicated RF ground links. Older systems used magnetic tape (>10 Gbit); modern ones use solid-state stores with higher capacity and faster access.
On-board computers: 32-bit microprocessors, microcontrollers or DSPs in radiation-tolerant technology. Software developed to high standards (object-oriented; Ada or ANSI C). High-integrity real-time operating systems (RTOS) are increasingly used.
Trend: from mission-specific software to off-the-shelf components and RTOS — saving development time and improving reliability, as requirements increasingly resemble terrestrial applications (autonomous decisions, intensive signal processing).

Compression and storage: send less, keep it safe until the next pass

Compression removes redundant or unnecessary information — e.g. eliminating unwanted information or reducing resolution in images.
On-linereal time: factor 2–8

Algorithm choice depends on the information needed and the reduction required; standards include JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) and CCSDS.
Storage: solid-state memory (dynamic RAM) instead of tape recorders, in block- or file-based systems. Error correction and scrubbing protect against radiation-induced errors (SEUs).

PART VII · ON-BOARD DATA HANDLING

Data downlinks: route, buffer, format, modulate, amplify — then fit it all into a few passes

Data handling for downlinks provides routing, buffering of different input/output rates (simple FIFO or a large data store), formatting, carrier modulation, amplification and generation of an RF beam.
QPSK is commonly used: a 100 Mbps stream needs about 55 MHz of RF bandwidth. X-band (8025–8400 MHz) for LEO supports around 500 Mbps but needs ITU permission — unlikely that the whole range is granted to one mission.
EIRP of typically 15 dBW normally needs travelling-wave-tube or solid-state amplifiers and an antenna with some gain — from fixed pseudo-omni reflectors to electronically steered beams. The beam must limit the power flux density per WARC regulations.
Link-layer processing turns source packets into CCSDS physical-channel PDUs: pseudo-randomization, differential encoding (phase ambiguity), forward error correction.

PART VIII · TECHNOLOGY & OUTLOOK

Space electronics must survive launch, vacuum, temperature — and radiation

Resources: mass, power and volume are critical; electronics must withstand launch vibration, temperature extremes and vacuum.
Packaging: avoid pressurized voids (common in plastic packages) and outgassing materials that could contaminate optical instruments.
Simple mitigation: catastrophic effects are much reduced when devices are unpowered — so power down subsystems when not required.
Single Event Upset (SEU) — temporary
Ionizing radiation changes the state of a node → noise, memory corruption, random software malfunctions. Mitigation: error-correction codes, memory scrubbing, voting logic (triple-module redundancy), watchdog timers.
Total dose — cumulative
Cumulative radiation causes permanent failure, measured in krad; required immunity from ~1 krad to ~100 krad. Mitigation: shielding by structure, equipment housing or special packaging.
Latch-up — destructive
A single ion triggers uncontrolled current flow that can destroy a device. Mitigation: shielding, or current sensing and limiting circuits that reset the device.
PART VIII · TECHNOLOGY & OUTLOOK

Space processors lag terrestrial ones — and that is usually fine

Lag of several years behind terrestrial technology is often sufficient for routine tasks: attitude control, housekeeping, systems management.
Simple 8-bit microcontrollers can be adequate for these tasks.
Demanding tasks: 32-bit processors and signal processors are available, but with limited processing power compared to terrestrial equivalents.
Peripherals are the problem: RAM, ROM and I/O controllers often need custom technology (limited width or serial interfaces). Some processors are space-qualified; the same cannot be said for peripheral devices.
Bus width must be chosen carefully: wider buses need more support devices → more complexity and size.
Custom logic: UARTs, dynamic-RAM controllers, I/O controllers and communications devices are usually implemented in field-programmable technology.

Three families of standards shape every TT&C designfrom [2]

ECSS — European Cooperation for Space Standardization
  • Single coherent set of standards for European space activities (ESA, CNES, DLR, ASI, UKSA… and industry).
  • Branches M, Q, E, U (+ S); TT&C lives in discipline E-50 Communications, e.g. ECSS-E-ST-50 (communications), -50-02 (ranging and Doppler), TC/TM modulation and coding standards.
  • Usually a sufficient starting set of requirements.
CCSDS — Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems
  • Inter-agency group (ESA, NASA, JAXA, Roscosmos…; 11 members, 32 observers) for interoperability and cross-support.
  • Blue books = standards, green books = informative reports, orange books = experimental.
  • Packet TM/TC, AOS, Proximity-1, CFDP — Parts II and VI of this lecture.
SFCG — Space Frequency Coordination Group
  • Recommendations where the ITU does not define allocations — e.g. lunar (REC 32-2) and Martian (REC 22-1) links.
  • Frequency coordination between agencies for orbit-to-orbit and surface-to-orbit links.
In practice: start from ECSS E-50, go to CCSDS for novel formats and protocols, and to ITU/SFCG for frequencies [2].
WRAP-UP · CHECK

Concept check

1. Why do both receivers of a basic TT&C subsystem stay ON all the time?
Hot redundancy: the spacecraft must always be commandable.
2. At 4096 bps with 8 frames of 64 eight-bit words, how often is a sub-commutated channel sampled?
Frame rate = 4096/512 = 8 frames/s; one sample every 8 frames = 1 /s.
3. What marks the end of a CLTU at the receiver?
The deliberately non-correctable tail sends the receiver from Decode back to Search.
4. Doubling the TM bit rate with everything else fixed changes the margin by…
Required P/N₀ = Eb/N₀ + 10 log Rb: +3 dB for twice the rate.
5. Why does tone ranging need several tones (or a code)?
Accuracy and ambiguity trade against each other — combine them.
