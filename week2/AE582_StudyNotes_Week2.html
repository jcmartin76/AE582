AE 582: Satellite Engineering

Study Notes: The Space Environment & Hazards

A spacecraft never flies through empty space. From the moment it separates from its launch vehicle it is immersed in a tenuous but violent environment: a hot plasma streaming from the Sun, the Earth's magnetic cavity and its trapped-radiation belts, a residual atmosphere that erodes and drags, hard vacuum, and a growing swarm of debris. Every one of these forces a design decision. These notes explain what that environment is, the physics that governs it, and the hazards it creates — with derivations you can expand, three interactive demos embedded right in the page, and questions whose answers reveal only when you click.

1. The Solar-Terrestrial Connection

1.1 The Sun and the Solar Wind

The Sun's visible surface (the photosphere) is about 5800 K, yet just above it the temperature rises to over a million kelvin in the corona. At that temperature the protons and electrons are moving so fast that the Sun's gravity cannot hold the outer corona down. In 1958 Eugene Parker showed that the only steady solution is one in which the corona continuously expands outward, accelerating through the speed of sound and becoming a supersonic flow — the solar wind.

The solar wind is a plasma: an ionised gas that is electrically quasi-neutral (equal positive and negative charge) but, unlike an ordinary gas, responds collectively to electric and magnetic fields. Its typical properties at Earth's distance (1 AU) are worth memorising as order-of-magnitude anchors:

  • Composition: ~95% protons and electrons, ~4% alpha particles (He²⁺), traces of heavier ions.
  • Speed: 400 km/s in the "slow" wind; 700–800 km/s in the "fast" wind that streams out of coronal holes.
  • Density: only ~5–10 particles per cm³ — a far better vacuum than any laboratory can make.
  • Temperature: ~10⁵ K, and it is collisionless: particles almost never hit one another over an astronomical unit.

Because it is a magnetised, collisionless plasma, the solar wind carries the Sun's influence — momentum, energy, and crucially its magnetic field — all the way out past the planets to the heliopause, roughly 120 AU away, where it finally meets the interstellar medium. For a spacecraft, the solar wind is the "weather system" of interplanetary space: it sets the plasma environment beyond the magnetosphere, and its gusts (from flares and coronal mass ejections) drive the geomagnetic storms that stress every satellite at Earth.

1.2 The Interplanetary Magnetic Field (IMF)

Why does the solar wind carry the Sun's magnetic field at all? Because a highly conducting plasma and a magnetic field are locked together — the "frozen-in flux" theorem (Alfvén). Intuitively: any attempt to move the field relative to the plasma would drive huge currents (the conductivity is nearly infinite), and those currents produce forces that oppose the relative motion. The practical result is that the field lines are dragged along wherever the plasma goes. So as the solar wind flows radially outward, it stretches the Sun's field out with it into interplanetary space; that dragged-out field is the IMF.

But the Sun also rotates (about once every 25 days). A parcel of wind flies straight out radially, yet its "root" on the Sun keeps rotating. The field line connecting the parcel back to its root therefore gets wound into a spiral — exactly like the stream of water from a rotating garden sprinkler curves into a spiral even though each droplet flies straight. This is the Parker spiral. At Earth the spiral has wound up to about 45° from the radial (Sun–Earth) direction.

Parker spiral angle (angle of the IMF from the radial direction): $$\tan\psi = \frac{\Omega\, r \cos\lambda}{v_{sw}} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \psi \approx 45^\circ \text{ at } 1\,\text{AU}$$
— the ~45° spiral at Earth

A parcel leaves the Sun radially at speed $v_{sw}$; its foot-point rotates at angular rate $\Omega$. In the time $t=r/v_{sw}$ needed to reach radius $r$, the foot rotates by $\Delta\phi=\Omega t$. The connecting field line is an Archimedean spiral, and its local angle from radial obeys

$$\tan\psi = \frac{r\,d\phi}{dr} = \frac{\Omega\,r}{v_{sw}}\quad(\text{ecliptic, }\lambda=0).$$

At 1 AU: $\Omega = 2.9\times10^{-6}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, $r = 1.5\times10^{11}$ m, $v_{sw}\approx 4\times10^{5}$ m/s, giving $\tan\psi\approx 1.1$, so $\psi\approx 47^\circ$. Closer to the Sun the spiral is nearly radial; far out (e.g. at Jupiter) it is almost circular.

The single most important property of the IMF for Earth is its north–south component, $B_z$. When $B_z$ points southward — opposite to the Earth's northward-pointing field on the dayside — the two fields can magnetically reconnect: the field lines break and re-join, opening a door that lets solar-wind energy and plasma pour into the magnetosphere. This is the trigger for magnetic storms and substorms, bright aurorae, and the injection of the energetic particles that inflate the radiation belts. A northward IMF, by contrast, leaves the magnetosphere "closed" and comparatively quiet.

Why space weather matters to you as a designer: a severe storm can charge surfaces to kilovolts, inflate the atmosphere (increasing drag and orbit decay), inject penetrating electrons that bury charge deep in dielectrics (internal charging), and raise single-event-upset rates. The chain always starts here — with a southward IMF reconnecting at the dayside magnetopause.

2. Atmosphere, Ionosphere & Magnetosphere

2.1 The Neutral Atmosphere

The atmosphere does not "end" at any altitude — it simply thins out, with density falling roughly exponentially. What changes with height is the temperature gradient, and its sign flips several times. Sunlight is absorbed at the ground (warming the troposphere), by ozone in the stratosphere (a warm layer), then the mesosphere cools again, and finally the thermosphere is heated to 500–1500 K by the absorption of solar extreme-ultraviolet radiation. These reversals are what define the named "spheres."

The rate of the exponential falloff is the scale height $H$ — the altitude increase over which the density drops by a factor of $e$. It comes straight from balancing pressure against gravity:

$$\rho(h)=\rho_0\,e^{-h/H},\qquad H=\frac{k_B T}{m g},\qquad \lambda_{mfp}=\frac{1}{n\sigma}$$
— the scale height $H$

Hydrostatic balance: the pressure gradient holds up the weight of the gas, $\dfrac{dP}{dh}=-\rho g$. For an ideal gas $P=n k_B T = \rho\,\dfrac{k_B T}{m}$, so $\rho=\dfrac{Pm}{k_BT}$ and

$$\frac{dP}{dh}=-\frac{Pmg}{k_BT}=-\frac{P}{H},\qquad H\equiv\frac{k_BT}{mg}.$$

Integrating gives $P=P_0e^{-h/H}$ (hence $\rho=\rho_0e^{-h/H}$). Larger $H$ (hotter gas, or lighter molecules) means a more gradual falloff. In the thermosphere ($T\sim1000$ K, atomic O, $g\approx8.9$): $H\approx58$ km.

A second quantity governs the character of the gas: the mean free path $\lambda_{mfp}=1/(n\sigma)$, the average distance a molecule travels between collisions. Near the ground it is tens of nanometres; above ~100 km it exceeds 1000 km. Once the mean free path is enormous, molecules essentially never collide — the gas becomes collisionless, ordinary fluid concepts (pressure, viscosity, sound) break down, and the different species stop mixing. Heavy molecules settle low and light ones (H, He) float to the top, forming the extended geocorona of hydrogen that reaches thousands of kilometres up.

2.2 The Ionosphere

Solar ultraviolet and X-ray photons are energetic enough to knock electrons off atmospheric atoms. The resulting plasma — free electrons and ions embedded in the neutral gas between ~60 and ~1000 km — is the ionosphere. Its density is set by a balance between production (photoionisation, which needs sunlight and neutrals) and loss (recombination), which is why it is strongly layered and strongly day/night dependent:

  • D region (~60–90 km): weakly ionised, present only in daytime. It absorbs HF radio (and vanishes at night, which is why distant AM stations come in after dark).
  • E region (~90–150 km): moderate density; supports some radio propagation and shows sporadic-E patches.
  • F region (~150–500 km): the densest layer, splitting into F1 and F2 by day. Its peak electron density reflects HF radio around the world and is the main contributor to GPS signal delay and scintillation.
Design relevance: the ionosphere refracts and delays radio and GPS signals (a major navigation error source), scintillates trans-ionospheric links, charges surfaces in the auroral zones, and is the plasma that LEO spacecraft and the ISS actually fly through. Above the F-peak the density falls into the plasmasphere and merges with the magnetosphere.

2.3 The Magnetosphere and its Boundaries

Deep inside the Earth, convecting molten iron acts as a dynamo and produces a roughly dipolar magnetic field. Out in space this field meets the supersonic solar wind. Because the solar wind is a magnetised plasma, it cannot simply mix with the Earth's field; instead it is deflected around it, carving out a comet-shaped cavity — the magnetosphere — that shields the planet from most of the direct solar-wind flux.

Three boundaries form where a supersonic flow meets an obstacle:

  • The bow shock: like the shock ahead of a supersonic aircraft, the flow is abruptly slowed from supersonic to subsonic and heated.
  • The magnetosheath: the turbulent, shocked, compressed solar-wind plasma between the bow shock and the magnetopause.
  • The magnetopause: the actual boundary of the Earth's field, where the solar-wind pressure pushing in balances the magnetic pressure pushing out. On the sunward side it sits near $10\,R_E$; on the night side the field is stretched into a long magnetotail that reaches past the Moon's orbit.
Magnetopause standoff distance (pressure balance): $$\rho_{sw}v_{sw}^2 \approx \frac{B_{mp}^2}{2\mu_0}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\frac{R_{mp}}{R_E}\approx\left(\frac{2B_0^2}{\mu_0\rho_{sw}v_{sw}^2}\right)^{1/6}\approx 10$$
— the standoff distance and why it barely moves

The wind's ram (dynamic) pressure $\rho_{sw}v_{sw}^2$ is balanced by the magnetic pressure of the compressed dipole. The dipole falls as $B(r)=B_0(R_E/r)^3$, and boundary currents roughly double it: $B_{mp}\approx 2B_0(R_E/R_{mp})^3$. Equating pressures,

$$\rho_{sw}v_{sw}^2=\frac{[2B_0(R_E/R_{mp})^3]^2}{2\mu_0}\;\Rightarrow\;\left(\frac{R_{mp}}{R_E}\right)^6=\frac{2B_0^2}{\mu_0\rho_{sw}v_{sw}^2}.$$

With nominal numbers this gives $R_{mp}\approx 9.7\,R_E$. The 1/6 power is the key: to halve the standoff distance you must raise the solar-wind pressure by $2^6=64\times$. That is why the magnetopause normally sits near $10\,R_E$ but, in the most extreme storms, can be pushed inside geostationary orbit ($6.6\,R_E$) — briefly exposing GEO satellites directly to the solar wind.

3. Charged-Particle Motion & Trapping

Below ~200 km the neutral gas dominates; above it, the plasma does, and a spacecraft's environment is set by how individual charged particles move in the Earth's field. That motion follows the Lorentz force. The trick that makes it tractable is to split the motion into a fast, tight gyration around a field line plus a slow drift of the centre of that circle (the "guiding centre").

$$m\frac{d\vec v}{dt}=q\left(\vec E+\vec v\times\vec B\right)+\vec F_{ext}$$

3.1 Gyration

Take the simplest case: a uniform $\vec B$, no electric field. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work (the speed is constant) and it bends the path into a circle. The particle therefore spirals around the field line: it circles in the plane perpendicular to $\vec B$ while sliding freely along $\vec B$, tracing a helix. Two numbers describe it — how fast it goes around (the gyrofrequency) and how big the circle is (the gyroradius):

$$\omega_c=\frac{qB}{m},\qquad r_g=\frac{mv_\perp}{qB}=\frac{v_\perp}{\omega_c}$$
— gyrofrequency and gyroradius

With $\vec B=B\hat z$, the cross product gives $\vec v\times\vec B=(v_yB,-v_xB,0)$, so

$$m\dot v_x=qBv_y,\qquad m\dot v_y=-qBv_x.$$

Differentiate the first and substitute the second: $\ddot v_x=-(qB/m)^2 v_x$ — simple harmonic motion at $\omega_c=qB/m$. Circular motion at that rate with rim speed $v_\perp$ has radius $r_g=v_\perp/\omega_c=mv_\perp/(qB)$.

Read these formulas physically. The gyroradius grows with momentum ($mv_\perp$) and shrinks with field strength: heavy or fast particles make wide circles, strong fields wind them tight. The gyrofrequency is independent of speed but depends on charge-to-mass ratio, so an electron gyrates about 1800 times faster than a proton in the same field. And because $q$ appears with its sign, electrons and ions circle in opposite senses. Across the regions a spacecraft meets, $r_g$ ranges from metres (electrons in a strong field) to thousands of kilometres (energetic ions in the weak outer field) — which is exactly why "the plasma" behaves so differently from place to place.

3.2 Guiding-centre drifts

If some other force acts in addition to $\vec B$, the circle's centre slowly drifts. The most important case is a perpendicular electric field, which produces the $\vec E\times\vec B$ drift. Its remarkable feature is that it is completely independent of charge and mass — protons and electrons drift together, at the same velocity, so no current flows. This is the drift that circulates plasma through the entire magnetosphere ("convection").

$$\vec v_{E\times B}=\frac{\vec E\times\vec B}{B^2}\qquad(\text{same for every particle})$$
— why the $E\times B$ drift is charge-independent

Look for a steady drift $\vec v_d$ that exactly cancels the electric force, leaving pure gyration: $0=q(\vec E+\vec v_d\times\vec B)$, i.e. $\vec E=-\vec v_d\times\vec B$. Cross with $\vec B$ and use $\vec v_d\perp\vec B$:

$$\vec E\times\vec B=-(\vec v_d\times\vec B)\times\vec B=\vec v_d B^2\;\Rightarrow\;\vec v_d=\frac{\vec E\times\vec B}{B^2}.$$

Both $q$ and $m$ cancel. By contrast, the gradient and curvature drifts (from a non-uniform field) scale as $\dfrac{mv^2}{qB^3}$ — they carry $1/q$, so electrons and ions drift in opposite directions. That charge separation is a current: it is precisely what forms the westward ring current that circles the Earth and depresses the surface field during magnetic storms.

QUICK CHECK
Under an $\vec E\times\vec B$ drift, how do protons and electrons behave?
Answer: A. $\vec v_{E\times B}$ has no $q$ or $m$, so no current results. It is the gradient/curvature drifts (which carry $1/q$) that separate charges and drive the ring current.

3.3 Magnetic mirroring and the loss cone

The Earth's field is not uniform — it gets stronger toward the poles, where the field lines crowd together. Watch what happens to a spiralling particle heading poleward. As it enters stronger field, a quantity called the magnetic moment $\mu=mv_\perp^2/2B$ stays almost constant (it is an adiabatic invariant — conserved whenever the field changes slowly compared with a gyration). Since $\mu$ is fixed and $B$ is rising, $v_\perp^2$ must rise too; but the total speed is fixed (the magnetic force does no work), so $v_\parallel$ must fall. The particle's forward motion slows, stops, and reverses: it has mirrored. Trapped particles therefore bounce back and forth between a northern and a southern mirror point.

$$\mu=\frac{mv_\perp^2}{2B}=\text{const},\qquad B_{\text{mirror}}=\frac{B_{eq}}{\sin^2\alpha_{eq}},\qquad \sin^2\alpha_{lc}=\frac{B_{min}}{B_{max}}$$
— mirror point and loss-cone angle

With $\mu$ and speed $v^2=v_\perp^2+v_\parallel^2$ both conserved, $v_\perp^2=\dfrac{2\mu}{m}B$ grows with $B$, so $v_\parallel^2=v^2-\dfrac{2\mu}{m}B$. The particle mirrors ($v_\parallel=0$) where $B=B_m=\dfrac{mv^2}{2\mu}$. Using the equatorial pitch angle $\alpha_{eq}$ (with $v_{\perp,eq}=v\sin\alpha_{eq}$) gives $\mu=\dfrac{mv^2\sin^2\alpha_{eq}}{2B_{eq}}$, hence

$$B_m=\frac{B_{eq}}{\sin^2\alpha_{eq}}.$$

A particle is lost if its mirror point would lie below the atmosphere, i.e. if it needs $B_m>B_{max}$ (the field at the top of the atmosphere). The marginal case $B_m=B_{max}$ defines the loss-cone half-angle: $\sin^2\alpha_{lc}=B_{eq}/B_{max}=B_{min}/B_{max}=1/R_m$. Particles with equatorial pitch angle inside the cone ($\alpha_{eq}<\alpha_{lc}$) plunge into the atmosphere; the rest bounce and stay trapped. The loss cone depends only on field geometry — not on the particle's energy or charge.

The particles that fall inside the loss cone precipitate into the upper atmosphere near the poles, deposit their energy, and light up the aurora. The particles outside it stay trapped for months or years — the raw material of the radiation belts. The mirror machine is thus both the aurora's cause and the belts' container.

4. The Radiation Belts

Now combine everything. A trapped particle does three periodic motions at once, on three very different timescales:

  • Gyration around the field line — microseconds to milliseconds.
  • Bounce between the northern and southern mirror points — tenths of a second to seconds.
  • Drift in longitude around the whole Earth — minutes to hours (electrons eastward, ions westward, from the gradient/curvature drift).

Because $\tau_{gyro}\ll\tau_{bounce}\ll\tau_{drift}$, each motion has its own conserved "adiabatic invariant," and together they confine the particle to a drift shell: the slow drift carries the bouncing particle all the way around the planet, sweeping its bounce path into a closed, doughnut-shaped shell. Fill many such shells with particles of many energies and you have the Van Allen radiation belts — the first discovery of the Space Age (Explorer 1, 1958).

There are two main belts. The inner belt ($L\lesssim2$, roughly 1000–6000 km) is dominated by very energetic protons; it is stable and long-lived, fed partly by cosmic-ray impacts on the atmosphere (CRAND). The outer belt ($L\sim3$–7, straddling GPS and GEO) is mostly relativistic electrons; it is highly dynamic, brightening and emptying within hours during storms as waves accelerate and scatter electrons. Between them lies the relatively empty slot region.

One more twist matters enormously in LEO. The Earth's dipole is tilted and offset from the planet's centre, so over the South Atlantic the inner belt dips down to just a few hundred kilometres — the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA). Low-orbiting spacecraft (including the ISS and most Earth-observation satellites) take the great majority of their radiation dose and single-event upsets during their brief passes through the SAA. It is common practice to safe sensitive instruments while crossing it.

Dipole field along a field line (magnetic latitude $\lambda$, shell parameter $L$): $$B(\lambda)=\frac{B_0}{L^3}\,\frac{\sqrt{1+3\sin^2\lambda}}{\cos^6\lambda}$$
Design relevance: where your orbit sits in $L$ decides your dose. LEO below the belts is mild except for the SAA; MEO and GTO transits push straight through the heart of the belts (harsh); GEO sits in the variable outer edge. This drives part selection, spot shielding, and radiation-tolerant electronics — the subject of §5.

5. Spacecraft Hazards

Everything above sets the stage; here is how it actually threatens hardware, and what designers do about it.

5.1 Micro-gravity

In free fall there is no "down," so buoyancy vanishes and natural convection stops. On the ground, hot fluid rises and cool fluid sinks; in orbit it does not, so heat and mass must be moved deliberately — by pumps, fans, wicks and heat pipes rather than by letting warmth rise. Fluids are governed instead by surface tension and by how they were last accelerated. Propellant does not sit obediently at the bottom of a tank; it clings to walls and forms floating blobs. Before an engine can be fired, the fuel must be settled over the outlet — with a small ullage thrust, a spinning motion, or capillary propellant-management devices (vanes and sponges) inside the tank. Micro-gravity therefore reaches into structures, propulsion, attitude control and thermal design at once.

5.2 The Vacuum Environment

Space is a hard vacuum (~$10^{-9}$ Pa or lower in GEO), and that emptiness causes several distinct problems:

  • Outgassing & contamination: materials slowly release trapped gases and volatiles, which re-condense on cold surfaces — fogging optics and darkening thermal coatings. Materials are screened for low outgassing (TML/CVCM limits).
  • Electrical breakdown (Paschen's curve): the voltage at which a gap arcs depends on pressure × gap distance and has a minimum at intermediate pressure. In a hard vacuum there are almost no molecules to ionise, so the breakdown voltage is very high. But during ascent or on-orbit venting the spacecraft passes through that dangerous mid-pressure range (around ~70 kft equivalent), where an electron can gain ionising energy between collisions and a self-sustaining arc forms at surprisingly low voltage. High-voltage systems must be sealed, potted, or kept out of that regime while venting. (A related in-vacuum arc, multipactor, plagues RF components.)
  • Cold welding: in air, every metal wears a thin oxide/adsorbed-gas layer that keeps surfaces from bonding. In vacuum those layers can be lost, and two clean metal surfaces pressed together may fuse — jamming mechanisms. Cold welding contributed to the partial failure of Galileo's high-gain antenna.
  • Metallic whiskers: tin and zinc coatings spontaneously grow hair-thin conductive filaments in vacuum, which can bridge conductors and short circuits. This is why aerospace electronics avoid pure-tin finishes.

5.3 The Neutral Atmosphere in LEO — Drag and Atomic Oxygen

Even at hundreds of kilometres there is enough residual atmosphere to matter. Two effects dominate:

Drag and orbital decay. The thin gas exerts a drag force that removes orbital energy, lowering the orbit until the satellite re-enters. How fast this happens depends on the ballistic coefficient $B_c=m/(C_DA)$ — a satellite's mass spread over its drag area. Heavy, compact spacecraft (large $B_c$) shrug off drag; light, high-area ones (CubeSats, or anything with deployed panels) decay quickly. Crucially, drag also varies by an order of magnitude over the 11-year solar cycle, because solar EUV heats and inflates the thermosphere near solar maximum — the same 400 km orbit can have a lifetime of years at solar minimum but months at solar maximum.

$$\vec F_D=-\tfrac12\rho v^2 C_D A\,\hat v,\qquad B_c=\frac{m}{C_DA},\qquad \frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau}\approx -3\pi\,\rho\,a\,\frac{C_DA}{m}\ \text{per orbit}$$
— bigger ballistic coefficient → longer life (and why drag "speeds you up")

The drag deceleration is $a_D=\dfrac{F_D}{m}=\dfrac{\tfrac12\rho v^2 C_DA}{m}=\dfrac{\rho v^2}{2B_c}$, so decay scales as $1/B_c$. A circular orbit has total energy $E=-\dfrac{\mu m}{2a}$ and speed $v=\sqrt{\mu/a}$. Drag removes energy, so $a$ decreases — but then $v=\sqrt{\mu/a}$ increases. Counter-intuitively, drag makes a satellite go faster as it spirals down (the lost potential energy more than pays for the gained kinetic energy). And since $\rho$ climbs steeply as the orbit lowers, decay accelerates catastrophically near the end — re-entry is abrupt.

Atomic oxygen (ATOX). Above ~100 km, solar UV splits molecular oxygen ($O_2$) into single, highly reactive oxygen atoms — the most abundant species at LEO altitudes. A spacecraft rams into these atoms at ~8 km/s, and the collisions chemically erode organic materials: polymer films (like Kapton), carbon composites, and silver interconnects are oxidised and worn away. Ram-facing surfaces are protected with atomic-oxygen-resistant coatings (e.g. $SiO_2$ overcoats) or robust materials.

5.4 The Radiation Environment

Energetic particles — trapped belt electrons and protons, solar energetic particles from flares/CMEs, and galactic cosmic rays — damage spacecraft in three distinct ways:

  • Total ionising dose (TID): the slow accumulation of absorbed dose gradually shifts transistor thresholds and degrades electronics over the mission. Countered by shielding and rad-tolerant parts.
  • Displacement damage: particles knock atoms out of the crystal lattice, degrading solar cells, sensors and optocouplers.
  • Single-event effects (SEE): a single heavy particle can flip a memory bit (SEU), trigger a latch-up (SEL, potentially destructive), or a transient (SET). These are random and cannot be shielded away entirely — they are handled with error-correcting codes, watchdogs, current-limiting and redundancy.

Shielding helps for dose but has limits: adding mass gives diminishing returns, and high-atomic-number shields generate secondary bremsstrahlung X-rays. Real designs combine modest shielding, careful part selection and qualification, spot-shielding of sensitive components, and fault-tolerant architecture.

5.5 Micrometeoroids and Orbital Debris (MMOD)

Finally, the mechanical threat. Micrometeoroids are natural dust; orbital debris is human-made — spent stages, dead satellites, and fragments from explosions and collisions. Both strike at hypervelocity (several to tens of km/s), and at those speeds even a tiny fragment carries enormous kinetic energy (a 1 mm aluminium fleck ≈ the energy of a fast baseball, concentrated on a pinhead). Impacts pit surfaces, puncture blankets and tanks, and can sever booms or destroy a spacecraft outright.

Designers respond with Whipple shields (a thin "bumper" standoff layer that vaporises an impactor before it reaches the wall), with conjunction tracking and collision-avoidance manoeuvres for larger objects, and with end-of-life disposal (de-orbit or graveyard orbits) to limit growth. The looming systemic risk is the Kessler syndrome: once the debris density is high enough, collisions generate more debris faster than it decays, and certain orbits could become a self-sustaining cascade — a strong argument for the 25-year (now moving to 5-year) post-mission disposal guidelines.

Worked Problem 5 — MMOD impact energy
Kinetic energy of a 1 mm aluminium sphere ($\rho=2700$ kg/m³) at 10 km/s?
$V=\tfrac43\pi(5\times10^{-4})^3=5.2\times10^{-10}$ m³; $m=\rho V=1.4\times10^{-6}$ kg.
$E=\tfrac12 mv^2=\tfrac12(1.4\times10^{-6})(10^4)^2$ $\approx 70$ J — a fast-pitched baseball's energy on a pinhead. Hence Whipple shields and avoidance.

1. According to Paschen's curve, what is the main electrical hazard while a spacecraft vents gas or crosses the mid-atmosphere (~70 kft)?
Answer: B. Paschen's curve has a minimum breakdown voltage at intermediate pressure×gap, where the mean free path lets electrons gain ionising energy yet collisions still build an avalanche. Avoid operating high-voltage systems in that regime while venting/ascending.
2. What creates the corrosive atomic-oxygen (ATOX) environment in LEO?
Answer: D. Above ~100 km solar UV splits $O_2$ into atomic O, the dominant LEO species. Ram impact of these atoms erodes polymers, silver and carbon — hence protective coatings on ram-facing surfaces.
3. A designer wants the slowest drag-driven decay. Which helps most?
Answer: B. Since $a_D=\rho v^2/(2B_c)$, larger $B_c$ decays slowest. More area or $C_D$ speeds decay; solar maximum inflates the atmosphere (higher $\rho$), also speeding decay.
4. Which pair of motions makes the belts a closed shell, not just a ring?
Answer: B. The bounce covers latitude along a field line; the drift covers longitude around Earth. Together they sweep a doughnut-shaped shell — the belt.
5. Why does the magnetopause standoff distance change so little even when the solar-wind pressure varies a lot?
Answer: B. From pressure balance, $R_{mp}\propto(\rho v^2)^{-1/6}$. To halve the standoff distance you need a $2^6=64\times$ pressure increase — so it normally stays near $10\,R_E$ and only extreme storms push it inside GEO.
